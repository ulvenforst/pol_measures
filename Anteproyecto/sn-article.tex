\begin{abstract}
Measuring opinion polarization requires distinguishing the formation of opposed groups from dispersion alone. This thesis studies the \emph{Minimum Effort to Consensus} (MEC), a parametric family that interprets polarization as the minimum effort needed to bring an opinion distribution to a common position. The model applies to distributions with finite, nonempty support in $[0,1]$. Its parameters $\alpha,\beta\geq1$ govern the influence of group size and distance from consensus. The analysis establishes existence of an optimal consensus point, uniqueness for $\beta>1$, and the identity with the minimum Earth Mover's Distance to consensus when $\alpha=\beta=1$. MEC satisfies the three original Esteban--Ray axioms for $\alpha>1$ and $\beta\geq1$, and a central-split monotonicity property for symmetric configurations. The theoretical results also distinguish the effects of moving a whole group and transferring part of it toward an extreme. A Python implementation supports comparisons on all five-point Likert histograms of $100$ agents, reduced to $2{,}299{,}726$ representatives under reflection, and on published expert ratings of $15$ hypothetical histograms. On the expert benchmark, $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,1.15}$ achieves Kendall's $\tau_B\approx0.886$, matching the concordance of $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}$ and exceeding that of the Van der Eijk and Tastle--Wierman indices. This benchmark also informed parameter selection, so the comparison does not establish performance on unseen profiles. The results connect an interpretation of polarization as resistance to consensus with axiomatic guarantees and strong agreement with expert judgments on the evaluated profiles.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction, Research Question, and Hypothesis}\label{sec:intro}

Public opinion can fracture into opposed camps. Some groups coalesce around one position, others around a rival stance, and the middle ground thins out. Polarization involves more than disagreement spread across individuals and concerns a geometric pattern in which sizeable blocs stand apart while remaining internally cohesive. The distinction matters because inequality metrics track how spread out a distribution is, whereas polarization asks whether that spread concentrates into a few opposed camps. Esteban and Ray \citep{esteban1994measurement} gave this distinction a formal basis by relating polarization to \emph{identification} within groups and \emph{alienation} between them.

Three intuitions anchor this approach: \textbf{Identification} concerns cohesion within a camp, \textbf{Alienation} concerns the distance between camps, and \textbf{Mass concentration} concerns the size of the groups occupying those positions. Together, these features distinguish opposed camps from a population whose opinions are merely spread across a scale.

Different measures give different meanings to this structure. The Esteban--Ray measure (ER) combines identification and alienation through a pairwise sum of antagonism between groups \citep{esteban1994measurement}. The Van der Eijk index (VdE) treats polarization as the complement of agreement under a unimodality criterion \citep{van2001measuring}. Tastle--Wierman Dissention (TWD) uses category probabilities and distances from the mean to assess ordinal dispersion \citep{tastle2007consensus}. Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) measures the cost of moving mass between distributions and has been used to study congruence between public and elite opinion \citep{lupu2017new}. The Koudenburg--Kiers--Kashima Index (KI) is fitted to expert judgments of polarization \citep{koudenburg2021new}. The problem is therefore not merely to assign a number to a distribution of opinions, but to say what that number means.

The \emph{Minimum Effort to Consensus} (MEC) interprets polarization as resistance to consensus. Under this interpretation, a population is highly polarized when much effort is needed to bring its members to a common position and weakly polarized when little effort is needed. This idea leads to an optimization problem in which, given an opinion distribution, we define its polarization as the minimum effort required to transform it into a consensus distribution. The minimum is taken over all possible consensus points, so the least costly position is determined by the distribution itself.

This thesis draws on the joint paper on MEC by \citet{aranda2026mec}, which I coauthored. My work on this research was carried out within the \emph{measures} subgroup of PROMUEVA at AVISPA, Universidad del Valle. The thesis presents the mathematical development, proofs, computational methods, and experimental findings of that research in relation to the objectives of the approved proposal.

Let $\pi\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}$ be an opinion distribution with finite, nonempty support $\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)=\{x\in[0,1]\mid\pi(x)>0\}$, where $\pi(x)$ is the mass at each occupied position $x$. Its total mass is $\Sigma\pi=\sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)}\pi(x)$. The masses need not sum to one. For $\alpha,\beta\geq1$, the effort required to reach a common position $y\in[0,1]$ and the minimum of that effort are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MEC}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(y)
  &= \sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)} \pi(x)^{\alpha}\lvert x-y\rvert^{\beta},\\
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi)
  &= \min_{y\in[0,1]} \mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(y).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The exponent $\alpha$ controls the weight assigned to group size and represents the identification dimension; when $\alpha>1$, larger groups receive more-than-proportional weight. The exponent $\beta$ controls how effort depends on distance from consensus and represents the alienation dimension; when $\beta>1$, the cost grows more than proportionally with displacement. These parameters describe the effort stipulated by the model.

The minimum exists because the effort function is continuous on the compact interval $[0,1]$. The minimization yields both a polarization value and an optimal consensus point $y^\star$, and when $\beta>1$, strict convexity makes this point unique. At $\beta=1$, the minimizers are the medians of the distribution with masses $\pi(x)^\alpha$, and several positions may minimize the effort. The consensus point may lie between categories of a Likert scale. In either case, an optimum describes the least costly consensus under the model, but it does not predict which position a population will adopt.

The empirical evaluation follows the two lines established in the proposal. Exhaustive computational comparisons score five-point Likert histograms with MEC and competing indices to identify agreements and differences in their rankings. Comparison with the published expert ratings of \citet{koudenburg2021new} assesses how closely each measure recovers the expert order. These comparisons complement the axiomatic analysis, which establishes MEC's behavior under specified changes to a distribution.

\paragraph{Research question.}
Does MEC provide a theoretically sound and empirically valid measure of polarization that satisfies core axiomatic requirements and aligns with expert judgments more closely than established alternatives?

To address this question, the study combines formal proofs with a reproducible Python implementation. The theoretical analysis concerns finite opinion distributions on a one-dimensional interval, while the main experiments use five-point scales. The proposal also includes regional illustrations, which are not part of the experiments reported in the paper. Opinion positions are represented numerically in $[0,1]$, with distance measured by $|x-y|$. The analysis does not model how opinions evolve over time.

\paragraph{Mathematical and computational results.}
The joint paper \citep{aranda2026mec} establishes that MEC satisfies the basic definition of a polarization measure for all $\alpha,\beta\geq1$. It is zero at consensus, and the distribution with half of the total mass at each endpoint maximizes MEC among distributions with that same total mass. This result is distinct from compliance with the three Esteban--Ray axioms.

For the power distribution defined by $\pi^\alpha(x)=\pi(x)^\alpha$, the structural analysis characterizes MEC as its total mass $\Sigma(\pi^\alpha)$ multiplied by the $L^\beta$-dispersion of its normalized version. For $\beta=1$, this dispersion is the mean absolute deviation about a median. For $\beta=2$, it is the variance. In particular, $\mathrm{MEC}_{1,2}(\pi)$ is the variance of the normalized opinion distribution multiplied by $\Sigma\pi$. Admissible reflections and shifts of the entire distribution preserve MEC. Scaling every mass by $\lambda>0$ multiplies its value by $\lambda^\alpha$, so comparisons between populations of different sizes require an explicit normalization convention.

The linear case $(\alpha,\beta)=(1,1)$ also has a transport interpretation. MEC equals the minimum Earth Mover's Distance to a consensus distribution with the same total mass, using $|x-y|$ as the transport cost per unit of mass. The paper establishes the geometric identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MEC-extremal-intro}
\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}(\pi)
  = \frac{\Sigma\pi}{2}-\mathrm{EMD}(\pi,\pi^\star),
\end{equation}
where $\pi^\star$ places half of $\Sigma\pi$ at each endpoint. For distributions with the same total mass, this identity shows that greater proximity to the balanced extreme distribution corresponds to greater polarization in the linear case \citep{aranda2026mec}.

The proposal anticipated that MEC would satisfy the core polarization axioms and central-split monotonicity for $\alpha\geq1$ and $\beta>1$, and would agree with expert judgments more closely than established alternatives. The joint paper establishes compliance with the three original Esteban--Ray axioms for $\alpha>1$ and $\beta\geq1$. Axiom~1 requires the strict inequality $\alpha>1$, whereas Axioms~2 and~3 hold throughout $\alpha,\beta\geq1$ \citep{aranda2026mec}.

The paper also establishes \emph{central-split monotonicity}, called \emph{Property~1}, for all $\alpha,\beta\geq1$. Under the symmetric configuration defined in Section~\ref{subsec:mec-central-split}, moving equal positive portions of central mass to positions at equal nonzero distances from the centre, while leaving positive mass there, strictly increases MEC. The property differs from Axiom~3 and is not implied by the three original ER axioms, as the paper's counterexample for $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}$ demonstrates \citep{aranda2026mec}.

Two further results of the joint study clarify the distinction between extremism and polarization, which the proposal included among its diagnostic comparisons. The \emph{Minority Principle} shows that, in a distribution with exactly two occupied positions and unequal positive masses, the smaller group contributes more of the total effort at an optimal consensus, for all $\alpha,\beta\geq1$ \citep{aranda2026mec}.

The paper's \emph{Tipping Point} method applies to an initial two-point distribution with $\alpha=\beta=2$. For a fixed destination to the right of both groups, it identifies the unique positive amount of mass whose transfer from the right-hand group leaves MEC equal to its original value. Smaller positive transfers reduce MEC, whereas larger transfers, up to the mass of that group, increase it. For these same parameter values and a general finite support below $1$, the sign of the change under a positive transfer from the rightmost occupied position to $1$ is governed by a cubic polynomial. These results show that transferring a small part of a group toward an extreme need not increase polarization. The paper distinguishes such partial transfers from moving an entire group away from consensus, for which its shifting-away theorem establishes a strict increase under the stated conditions \citep{aranda2026mec}.

The convex formulation also supports efficient computation. For $\beta>1$, bisection on the derivative locates the optimal consensus point with positional accuracy $\varepsilon$ in $O(n\log(1/\varepsilon))$ time, where $n$ is the number of occupied positions. For $\beta=1$, the weighted-median characterization gives the minimizers directly. These mathematical procedures provide the basis for implementing and checking the computation required by the proposal.

\paragraph{Empirical findings.}
The computational comparison covers all five-point histograms with nonnegative integer counts summing to $100$. Retaining one representative per reflection class gives $2{,}299{,}726$ representatives from $4{,}598{,}126$ histograms. Kendall's $\tau_B$ measures concordance between the candidate indices over this corpus, accounting for tied scores.

The expert comparison reuses published ratings of fifteen hypothetical histograms \citep{koudenburg2021new} to assess KI and the other indices against the expert order. On these profiles, $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,1.15}$ attains Kendall's $\tau_B\approx0.886$, matching the concordance of $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}$ and exceeding that of VdE and TWD \citep{aranda2026mec}. The hypothesis of improved agreement is therefore only partly supported. These same profiles informed parameter selection, and several MEC parameter pairs attained the same concordance. Agreement on this benchmark does not establish performance on unseen profiles.

The thesis first reviews the conceptual background and competing measures, then states the objectives and scope. The definition and structural properties of MEC lead into the theoretical results and axiomatic analysis. The computational methodology and implementation provide the basis for the experimental results. The discussion examines the evidence, its limitations, and fulfillment of the approved objectives. The thesis closes with conclusions and directions for future work.

\section[Literature Review and Conceptual Framework]{Literature Review and\\Conceptual Framework}\label{sec:lit}
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{2em}

Distinguishing polarization from dispersion and extremism clarifies what an index is intended to measure. Dispersion concerns how far opinions spread around a reference position. Extremism concerns their proximity to the ends of a scale. Polarization concerns the size, cohesion, and separation of groups, following the identification--alienation framework of Esteban and Ray \citep{esteban1994measurement}. A population concentrated at one extreme can therefore exhibit extremism while remaining in consensus. Conversely, two opposed groups need not occupy the endpoints to be polarized. Moving a whole group toward an extreme can affect polarization differently from transferring only part of its mass \citep{aranda2026mec}.

Likert-type data provide ordered categories, such as levels of agreement with a proposition. A distance-based measure also requires numerical coordinates for those categories. The principal experiments use five equally spaced positions. The distances depend on that coding, since category order alone does not determine numerical separation. MEC's value alone does not establish affective hostility or network segregation.

Consider an opinion distribution $\pi$ supported on the $m$ fixed categories of an ordered scale. Write their numerical positions as $x_1<\cdots<x_m$ in $[0,1]$ and their masses as $\pi_i=\pi(x_i)$. Empty categories have $\pi_i=0$. The total mass, category probabilities, and mean are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lit-probabilities}
M=\Sigma\pi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_i>0,
\qquad
p_i=\frac{\pi_i}{M},
\qquad
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_i x_i.
\end{equation}
The normalized distribution is $\overline{\pi}(x)=\pi(x)/M$ for $x\in[0,1]$. Thus $p_i=\overline{\pi}(x_i)$ are probabilities, while $\pi_i$ may be counts or other nonnegative masses.

\subsection{Identification, Alienation, and the Axiomatic Tradition}\label{subsec:lit-axioms}

The Esteban--Ray index sums effective antagonism between pairs of groups \citep{esteban1994measurement}. Denoting its parameter by $\alpha_{\mathrm{ER}}$, the measure is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lit-er}
\mathrm{ER}_{\alpha_{\mathrm{ER}}}(\pi)
=K\sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)}
   \sum_{z\in\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)}
   \pi(x)^{1+\alpha_{\mathrm{ER}}}\pi(z)\lvert x-z\rvert,
\end{equation}
where $K>0$ is a normalization constant and $\alpha_{\mathrm{ER}}\in(0,\alpha^\ast]$, with $\alpha^\ast\approx1.6$. Its mass exponent is $1+\alpha_{\mathrm{ER}}$, compared with $\alpha$ in MEC. The factor involving group mass gives greater weight to identification in larger groups, while the distance term enters linearly. A balanced distribution at the two endpoints is maximally polarized under this formulation among distributions with the same total mass.

Esteban and Ray's three axioms specify changes under which polarization should increase. The first concerns merging two groups of equal mass at their midpoint, when they are sufficiently close to one another and sufficiently small relative to a third group. The second concerns moving an intermediate group away from the larger endpoint group and toward the smaller one, to which it is already closer. The third concerns transferring equal portions of central mass to two equally sized, symmetrically placed outer groups. Each axiom has conditions on masses, positions, and the permitted transformation. These conditions do not imply that every merger or every movement toward an extreme increases polarization.

Satisfying these axioms does not by itself characterize the form of a measure. Kawada, Nakamura, and Sunada (KNS) \citep{kawada2018characterization} identified a counterexample to the original Esteban--Ray characterization and restored the result by strengthening the first axiom. Their characterization applies to measures expressed as pairwise sums of effective antagonism and requires rankings to be preserved when all masses are multiplied by a common positive factor, a property called Condition~H. MEC satisfies this condition and, under the parameter conditions summarized in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, the three original axioms \citep{aranda2026mec}. These results alone do not establish that MEC belongs to the class covered by the corrected characterization.

The paper also draws on Social Identity Theory, which relates part of an individual's self-conception to membership in social groups \citep{TajfelTurner1979}. Opinions can express such affiliations, so substantial opinion changes may also concern group identity. The theory of cognitive dissonance provides a further reason to consider the cost of revising established beliefs \citep{Festinger1957}. These ideas motivate the possibility that large ideological shifts entail costs that grow more than proportionally with distance, as represented in MEC when $\beta>1$. This interpretation does not make MEC's weights and costs direct measurements of psychological identification or dissonance.

\subsection{Agreement and Dissention on Ordered Scales}\label{subsec:lit-ordinal}

The axiomatic tradition assesses polarization through specified changes in group structure. Agreement measures approach the same distributions by asking how responses concentrate around shared positions. Two relevant approaches are Van der Eijk's agreement measure and Tastle--Wierman Dissention.

\paragraph{Van der Eijk.}
Van der Eijk \citep{van2001measuring} measures agreement through the patterns of unimodality in an ordered rating scale, and its interpretation assumes equally spaced categories. The construction decomposes a histogram into semiuniform layers. At each step, a layer assigns the smallest positive remaining mass to every category whose remaining mass is positive, so each layer has the same positive mass at its occupied categories and zero mass elsewhere. Subtracting that layer and repeating the procedure exhausts the histogram. A binary vector $P_\ell$ records which categories are occupied in layer $\ell$.

For $m\geq3$, consider all triples of category indices $1\leq i<j<k\leq m$. Let $TU_\ell$ count those for which $(P_{\ell,i},P_{\ell,j},P_{\ell,k})$ is $110$ or $011$, and let $TDU_\ell$ count those with pattern $101$. The latter pattern places an empty category between two occupied categories. If $TU_\ell+TDU_\ell>0$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lit-vde-layer}
\begin{aligned}
U_\ell
 &=\frac{(m-2)TU_\ell-(m-1)TDU_\ell}
         {(m-2)(TU_\ell+TDU_\ell)},\\
A_\ell
 &=U_\ell\left(1-\frac{S_\ell-1}{m-1}\right),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $S_\ell$ is the number of occupied categories in that layer. When both triple counts are zero, the convention is $U_\ell=1$. This also makes the expression well defined for layers representing consensus or a uniform distribution.

Let $w_\ell$ be the fraction of total mass contained in layer $\ell$, so that $\sum_\ell w_\ell=1$. The agreement of the complete distribution and its polarization transformation are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lit-vde}
A(\pi)=\sum_\ell w_\ell A_\ell,
\qquad
\mathrm{VdE}(\pi)=\frac{1-A(\pi)}{2}.
\end{equation}
The agreement score lies in $[-1,1]$ and the transformed score lies in $[0,1]$. The division by two specifies the scale used in the implementation. Van der Eijk's construction therefore responds to the arrangement of occupied categories and to patterns of separation, rather than simply measuring distance from a mean.

\paragraph{Tastle--Wierman.}
Tastle and Wierman \citep{tastle2007consensus} use information-theoretic ideas to define consensus and dissention on ordinal scales, and their construction combines category probabilities with distances from the mean. With the probabilities and mean in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lit-probabilities}, let $d=x_m-x_1>0$ be the amplitude of the fixed numerical scale. Their scores are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lit-twd}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\mathrm{TW}}(\pi)
 &=1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_i
       \log_2\left(1-\frac{|x_i-\mu|}{d}\right),\\
\mathrm{TWD}(\pi)
 &=1-C_{\mathrm{TW}}(\pi)
  =-\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_i
       \log_2\left(1-\frac{|x_i-\mu|}{d}\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Categories with $p_i=0$ contribute zero, including the limiting case in which the logarithm's argument is zero. The amplitude is that of the scale, not merely the range of its occupied categories. At consensus, all positive mass is at the mean, so $C_{\mathrm{TW}}=1$ and $\mathrm{TWD}=0$. For equal masses at the two endpoints, $C_{\mathrm{TW}}=0$ and $\mathrm{TWD}=1$.

Both VdE and TWD distinguish a uniform distribution from a balanced split at the endpoints. The paper compares their rankings with those of MEC and the other candidate measures across a wider range of profiles \citep{aranda2026mec}.

\subsection{Transport Distances and Consensus}\label{subsec:lit-transport}

The ordinal measures above describe agreement or dissention within a histogram. Transport geometry compares distributions through the least work required to move one mass configuration into another. The cost per unit of transported mass is the distance between its initial and final positions. On probability distributions on the line, this minimum cost is the $1$-Wasserstein distance, also called Earth Mover's Distance \citep{vallender1974calculation,geher2020isometric}. The paper applies the transportation formulation to finite opinion distributions of equal total mass \citep{aranda2026mec}.

As a consensus baseline, let $\pi_y$ place all of $M=\Sigma\pi$ at $y\in[0,1]$. Every occupied position must send its mass to $y$, so the transportation cost is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lit-emd-consensus}
\mathrm{EMD}(\pi,\pi_y)
=\sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)}\pi(x)|x-y|
=\mathrm{EC}_{1,1}^{\pi}(y).
\end{equation}
Minimizing over the possible consensus positions gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lit-emd-minimum}
\min_{y\in[0,1]}\mathrm{EMD}(\pi,\pi_y)
=\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}(\pi).
\end{equation}
Thus a cost to a fixed point and a minimum cost to consensus are different quantities, since the former depends on the chosen target, while the latter determines its optimal targets from the distribution. In this linear case, any median of $\pi$ is an optimal consensus point, and the minimum equals $M$ times the mean absolute deviation of $\overline{\pi}$ about a median. This is distinct from the median absolute deviation.

Beyond consensus baselines, Lupu, Selios, and Warner \citep{lupu2017new} use EMD to quantify congruence between public and elite opinion by comparing two distributions. In the present study, the benchmark labelled EMD is the minimum distance to consensus in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lit-emd-minimum}, which coincides with $\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}$. The geometric identity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:MEC-extremal-intro} also relates this minimum to proximity to the balanced extreme distribution.

Transport respects the locations and masses of the groups being moved, but a scalar cost does not uniquely determine a distribution's shape. Different profiles can have the same cost, and sharing a mean does not imply sharing a transport distance. The linear consensus cost also weights every group proportionally to its mass. The generalized MEC family changes this weighting through $\alpha$ and changes the dependence on displacement through $\beta$. Its weighted optimization retains the transport motivation without being identified, for arbitrary parameters, with an ordinary Wasserstein metric on the original distribution.

\subsection{Expert Judgments and KI}\label{subsec:lit-experts}

Expert judgments provide a reference for assessing whether an index's rankings agree with informed views of polarization. Koudenburg, Kiers, and Kashima \citep{koudenburg2021new} asked sixty researchers to rate fifteen hypothetical five-point histograms from $0$, meaning not polarized, to $100$, meaning maximally polarized. After data screening, their quantitative analyses used responses from fifty-eight experts.

The same study developed a regression-based index that weights pairs of category frequencies to reproduce expert assessments. We follow the paper in calling this measure the Koudenburg--Kiers--Kashima Index (KI). For masses $\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_5$ with total $M>0$, its expression on the original $0$--$100$ scale is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lit-ki}
\mathrm{KI}_{100}(\pi)
=\frac{2.14\,\pi_2\pi_4
       +2.70\,(\pi_1\pi_4+\pi_2\pi_5)
       +3.96\,\pi_1\pi_5}{0.0099\,M^2}.
\end{equation}
The implementation uses $\mathrm{KI}(\pi)=\mathrm{KI}_{100}(\pi)/100$ to express the score in $[0,1]$. Here $\pi_i$ denotes a frequency or mass, not a numerical opinion position. The products in the formula concern pairs of categories within a histogram.

The positive terms connect categories on opposite sides of the neutral category. Consequently, KI is zero whenever $\pi_1=\pi_2=0$ or $\pi_4=\pi_5=0$, even when several categories on the remaining side are occupied. The paper identifies this as a limitation of KI relative to the broader range of non-consensus configurations considered in its analysis \citep{aranda2026mec}. This feature reflects the index's emphasis on opposition across a neutral point. It should not be confused with a claim that every non-consensus profile is equally polarized.

KI is therefore a candidate measure, while the observed expert scores supply the reference ordering. The MEC study reuses those scores and compares the candidates using Kendall's $\tau_B$, which accounts for ties. The data consist of ratings of individual histograms, rather than judgments of pairs of histograms.

The benchmark also informed MEC's parameter selection, so agreement on these profiles does not establish performance on unseen distributions. Sections~\ref{sec:methods} and~\ref{sec:results} describe the comparison protocol and findings.



The thesis assesses this construction through formal and empirical criteria. The original ER axioms test specified changes in group structure. The paper's central-split property adds a criterion not implied by those axioms, as shown in Section~\ref{subsec:mec-central-split}. Concordance with other measures describes similarities between rankings, while comparison with expert scores assesses agreement with human judgments on the benchmark. These distinct questions guide the objectives in the next section.

\section{Objectives and Scope}\label{sec:objectives}

The purpose of this thesis is to establish what MEC measures, how it can be computed, and what the theoretical and empirical evidence supports. The approved proposal framed this purpose through five specific objectives, which are retained below as the basis for assessing the completed research. The joint paper supplies its principal mathematical and experimental results \citep{aranda2026mec}; the proposal also specifies software deliverables, supplementary analyses, and regional illustrations whose fulfillment must be assessed separately.

The general objective is to establish the mathematical foundations of the Minimum Effort to Consensus, provide a reproducible implementation, and assess its usefulness as a measure of opinion polarization through axiomatic analysis, computational comparisons, and published expert judgments. The aim is to determine the conditions under which MEC supports this interpretation and the limits of the resulting evidence.

This purpose is developed through five specific objectives that connect formal theory, computation, and empirical assessment.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Formal theory.} Establish the existence of optimal consensus points and their uniqueness when $\beta>1$. Prove the relation between $\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}$ and the minimum Earth Mover's Distance to consensus. Determine compliance with the Esteban--Ray axioms under explicit parameter conditions, and examine additional properties, including central-split monotonicity. The literature review supplies the conceptual basis for these requirements.
  \item \textbf{Synthetic universe.} Construct the exhaustive corpus of five-point histograms with nonnegative integer counts summing to $100$, retaining one representative per reflection class. Define consistent conventions for masses, positions, and normalization, and implement the competing measures. The comparison includes ER, VdE, TWD, and the EMD-to-consensus baseline envisaged in the proposal, together with KI as used in the completed paper.
  \item \textbf{Software.} Provide an open Python implementation in the \texttt{pol\_measures} repository, with a documented application programming interface, a command-line interface, unit tests, and reproducible execution procedures. Record numerical settings and use fixed random seeds wherever a procedure involves randomness.
  \item \textbf{Computational evaluation.} Examine MEC across specified parameter choices and compare its rankings with the candidate measures through concordance surfaces and Kendall's $\tau_B$. The proposal also envisaged top-$k$ overlap and diagnostic profiles contrasting dispersed opinion with opposed camps, extremism with polarization, and movements of whole groups with transfers of small portions of mass.
  \item \textbf{Expert comparison and regional illustration.} Assess agreement with the published scores for the fifteen hypothetical histograms of \citet{koudenburg2021new}, without collecting new expert judgments. Contribute to descriptive readings of small aggregate Likert-type cases from Valle del Cauca within the PROMUEVA team's regional applications, as envisaged in the approved proposal.
\end{enumerate}

The theoretical objectives concern the finite-distribution model of Eq.~\eqref{eq:MEC}, with $\alpha,\beta\geq1$ and no requirement of unit total mass. Following the distinction in Section~\ref{subsec:lit-axioms}, the analysis tests the three original Esteban--Ray axioms and central-split monotonicity under their respective hypotheses. It does not claim the later Kawada--Nakamura--Sunada characterization.

The main computational comparisons use five equally spaced opinion positions. The exhaustive corpus fixes total mass at $100$; it does not cover every finite distribution in the theoretical domain. Parameter searches examine the settings actually evaluated, and do not establish an optimum over the entire continuous parameter space. Because distance depends on numerical coordinates, the interpretation of a score is conditional on the chosen representation of the ordered categories.

The expert comparison is a secondary analysis of published histogram scores and evaluates agreement on those profiles. The paper's reference parametrization, $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,1.15}$, was selected with reference to the same benchmark used to report concordance. That comparison therefore does not establish independent validation on unseen profiles. The regional component specified in the proposal concerns descriptive readings of aggregate opinion distributions. It does not seek population-representative estimates or causal conclusions about civic issues or interventions. Comparisons across time require corresponding aggregate observations; they are not forecasts derived from the index.

The thesis studies measurement rather than opinion dynamics. It does not develop a network model, a multidimensional opinion measure, or a method for predicting when consensus will occur. The optimal point $y^\star$ is a least costly consensus position under the effort model, but it is not a prediction of the position a population will adopt. The Minority Principle and Tipping Point analysis already developed in the paper provide further interpretations of this static model under their stated assumptions.

\section{Minimum Effort to Consensus and Structural Properties}\label{sec:mec-foundations}

The preceding sections introduced MEC and established the criteria by which it will be assessed. We now develop its mathematical construction. The argument begins with opinion distributions and transportation to consensus, then establishes the properties that make the minimum well defined and interpretable. Convexity, the characterization through weighted dispersion, symmetry, and population scaling also provide the basis for subsequent computation. The definitions and results follow the completed paper \citep{aranda2026mec}, with their proofs presented alongside the statements.

\subsection{Opinion Distributions and Consensus}\label{subsec:mec-distributions}

Let $\mathcal{D}$ denote the opinion distributions introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, and $\mathcal{D}_M$ those with total mass $M>0$. An ordered vector representation is
\[
\pi=((\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_n),(x_1,\ldots,x_n)),
\qquad 0\leq x_1<\cdots<x_n\leq1.
\]
Here $\pi(x_i)=\pi_i\geq0$, $\sum_i\pi_i=M$, and $\pi(x)=0$ elsewhere. Zero entries may record unoccupied categories; only positive entries belong to the support. Counts, weights, and probabilities therefore share the same representation, although their total masses differ.

\begin{definition}[Power and normalized distributions]\label{def:mec-power}
For $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ and $\alpha\geq1$, the power distribution is $\pi^\alpha(x)=\pi(x)^\alpha$. The normalized distribution is $\overline{\pi}(x)=\pi(x)/\Sigma\pi$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-power-normalization}
\Sigma(\pi^\alpha)=\sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)}\pi(x)^\alpha,
\qquad
\overline{\pi^\alpha}(x)=\frac{\pi(x)^\alpha}{\Sigma(\pi^\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
The power operation preserves occupied positions and changes their relative weights. When $\alpha=1$, $\overline{\pi^\alpha}=\overline{\pi}$. For $\alpha>1$, optimal consensus will generally depend on the transformed weights rather than on the original frequencies.

\begin{definition}[Consensus and extremal distributions]\label{def:mec-consensus-extremal}
A distribution is a consensus at $y\in[0,1]$ if its support is $\{y\}$. For $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$, write $\pi_y$ for the consensus at $y$ with mass $\Sigma\pi$. Write $\pi^\star$ for the distribution with mass $\Sigma\pi/2$ at each endpoint $0$ and $1$, and zero mass elsewhere. Thus $\pi$, $\pi_y$, and $\pi^\star$ have the same total mass.
\end{definition}

A median $m\in[0,1]$ of a distribution $\rho\in\mathcal{D}$ satisfies
\[
\sum_{x\leq m}\rho(x)\geq\frac{\Sigma\rho}{2},
\qquad
\sum_{x\geq m}\rho(x)\geq\frac{\Sigma\rho}{2}.
\]
The set of medians is a nonempty closed interval, which may reduce to a single point. These inequalities allow more than one median, including points between occupied positions. Normalization preserves the set of medians, since it multiplies all masses by the same positive constant.

\begin{definition}[Polarization measure]\label{def:mec-polarization-measure}
A function $P\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}$ is a polarization measure in the sense used in the paper if it is zero at every consensus distribution and, for every $M>0$, an extremal distribution maximizes $P$ on $\mathcal{D}_M$.
\end{definition}
The three Esteban--Ray axioms impose further requirements on changes to group structure, as discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:lit-axioms}. The construction and structural properties developed here provide the basis for the maximality and axiomatic analysis that follows.

\subsection{From Transportation to Generalized MEC}\label{subsec:mec-transport-construction}

Section~\ref{subsec:lit-transport} described transportation as work required to move opinion mass. A transportation plan makes that interpretation precise. Let $\rho,\eta\in\mathcal{D}$ have the same total mass. A plan is a nonnegative function $\gamma$ supported on $\boldsymbol{s}(\rho)\times\boldsymbol{s}(\eta)$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-transport-marginals}
\sum_{z\in\boldsymbol{s}(\eta)}\gamma(x,z)=\rho(x),
\qquad
\sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\rho)}\gamma(x,z)=\eta(z).
\end{equation}
The first equality accounts for all mass leaving each source position; the second accounts for all mass arriving at each target. With distance as the cost per unit of mass, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-transport-definition}
\mathrm{EMD}(\rho,\eta)
=\min_{\gamma}\sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\rho)}
                     \sum_{z\in\boldsymbol{s}(\eta)}\gamma(x,z)|x-z|,
\end{equation}
where the minimum ranges over the plans satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:mec-transport-marginals}. Equal total mass ensures that a plan exists. The feasible set is a nonempty compact set of finitely many nonnegative entries, and the cost is continuous, so its minimum is attained.

\begin{lemma}[Transportation to consensus]\label{lem:mec-consensus-transport}
For every $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ and $y\in[0,1]$, the cost of transporting $\pi$ to $\pi_y$ equals the linear effort in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lit-emd-consensus}. Consequently, its minimum over all consensus positions is $\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}(\pi)$, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lit-emd-minimum}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A plan from $\pi$ to $\pi_y$ can send mass only to $y$. The source constraints therefore force $\gamma(x,y)=\pi(x)$ for every $x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)$. This unique plan has cost $\sum_x\pi(x)|x-y|$, which is $\mathrm{EC}_{1,1}^{\pi}(y)$. Minimizing over $y$ proves the second claim.
\end{proof}

The linear construction makes each group's contribution proportional to its mass and its displacement. The generalized model changes these two dependencies separately. For positive masses $p>q$, their weights have ratio $(p/q)^\alpha$, while for positive displacements $d_2>d_1$, their costs have ratio $(d_2/d_1)^\beta$. Thus $\alpha>1$ amplifies the relative influence of larger groups, and $\beta>1$ amplifies the relative cost of longer moves. The conceptual motivation for these choices was given in Section~\ref{subsec:lit-axioms}; here their roles enter the effort function explicitly.

\begin{definition}[Generalized MEC]\label{def:mec-generalized}
Let $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ and $\alpha,\beta\geq1$. The effort function $\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}$ and its minimum $\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi)$ are given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:MEC}. A point at which that minimum is attained is an $(\alpha,\beta)$-optimal consensus point. When the parameters are fixed, we omit them from this description and write $y^\star$ for an optimal point.
\end{definition}
The possible consensus positions fill $[0,1]$, even when the observed opinions occupy only a few categories. The minimum is therefore taken over a continuous interval. The identity with ordinary EMD on the original distribution concerns $(\alpha,\beta)=(1,1)$; it is not asserted for arbitrary parameters.

\subsection{Existence, Convexity, and Optimal Consensus}\label{subsec:mec-convexity}

The proposal identified existence and uniqueness as prerequisites for a reliable computation of MEC. Existence follows from continuity, whereas uniqueness requires a condition on the distance exponent.

\begin{proposition}[Existence of optimal consensus]\label{prop:mec-existence}
For every $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ and $\alpha,\beta\geq1$, the effort attains a minimum on $[0,1]$. Every optimal consensus point lies in the interval between the smallest and largest occupied opinions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The effort is a finite sum of continuous functions, so the extreme value theorem gives a minimum on the compact interval $[0,1]$. Let $a=\min\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)$ and $b=\max\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)$. If $y<a$, replacing $y$ by $a$ strictly reduces every distance to an occupied position. If $y>b$, replacing it by $b$ does the same. Positive weights and $\beta\geq1$ then strictly reduce the effort. Neither region can contain a minimizer.
\end{proof}

Existence also makes the zero value precise. MEC is zero if and only if the distribution is a consensus. At consensus, placing the target at the occupied opinion gives zero effort. Conversely, if the attained minimum is zero, every nonnegative term in the effort must vanish. Since each occupied position has positive weight, every such position must coincide with the minimizing target. Thus the support contains exactly one point. This establishes the first requirement of Definition~\ref{def:mec-polarization-measure} for all $\alpha,\beta\geq1$.

\begin{theorem}[Convexity of effort]\label{th:mec-convexity}
For every $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ and $\alpha,\beta\geq1$, $\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}$ is convex. If $\beta>1$, it is strictly convex and has a unique optimal consensus point.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a fixed $x$, the function $y\mapsto|x-y|^\beta$ is convex for $\beta\geq1$ and strictly convex for $\beta>1$ \citep{Rockafellar1970}. Multiplication by $\pi(x)^\alpha>0$ preserves these properties. Their finite sum is therefore convex, and strictly convex when $\beta>1$. A strictly convex function cannot have two distinct minimizers, since its value at their midpoint would be smaller. Existence was established in Proposition~\ref{prop:mec-existence}.
\end{proof}

Convexity ensures that a local minimum is global. For $\beta>1$, it also gives a direct characterization of the optimum through the balance function envisaged in the proposal,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-balance}
F_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(y)
=\sum_{x<y}\pi(x)^\alpha(y-x)^{\beta-1}
 -\sum_{x>y}\pi(x)^\alpha(x-y)^{\beta-1},
\end{equation}
where the sums range over occupied opinions. Differentiating the effort gives
\[
\frac{d}{dy}\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(y)
=\beta F_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(y).
\]
The contribution of an opinion at $y$ to the derivative is zero. Each signed term in $F$ is continuous and strictly increasing when $\beta>1$, so $F$ has the same properties. If the support contains more than one point, $F(a)<0$ and $F(b)>0$. Its unique zero lies in $(a,b)$ and is $y^\star$. At consensus, the zero is the single occupied position. Thus
\[
F_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(y^\star)=0
\]
characterizes the optimum in both cases.

This is the mathematical basis of bisection. For a support of size $n$, an evaluation of $F$ requires $O(n)$ operations. Bisection locates the zero with positional accuracy $\varepsilon$ in $O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations, giving $O(n\log(1/\varepsilon))$ time. These bounds concern the search for the consensus position. Numerical tolerances, implementation choices, and comparisons with a bounded scalar optimizer belong to the computational methodology. At $\beta=1$, the derivative argument does not apply at occupied positions; the median characterization below supplies the minimizers instead.

\subsection{Weighted Dispersion and Special Cases}\label{subsec:mec-dispersion}

The transformation of masses in Definition~\ref{def:mec-power} relates the effort problem to familiar measures of dispersion. For a finitely supported probability distribution $\rho$ and $p\geq1$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-lp-dispersion}
D_p(\rho)=\min_{y\in[0,1]}
          \sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\rho)}\rho(x)|x-y|^p.
\end{equation}
The reference position is chosen to minimize the expected $p$th power of displacement. This is the $L^p$-dispersion used in the paper.

\begin{theorem}[$L^\beta$-dispersion characterization]\label{th:mec-dispersion}
For every $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ and $\alpha,\beta\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-dispersion-characterization}
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi)
=\Sigma(\pi^\alpha)\,D_\beta(\overline{\pi^\alpha}).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Factoring out the positive transformed mass gives
\[
\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(y)
=\Sigma(\pi^\alpha)
 \sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)}\overline{\pi^\alpha}(x)|x-y|^\beta.
\]
The factor is independent of $y$. Taking minima and applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:mec-lp-dispersion} proves the identity. The two objectives also have the same minimizers.
\end{proof}

For $\beta=1$ and $\beta=2$, the characterization identifies both the optimum and its value. Let $\rho=\overline{\pi^\alpha}$. Write $m_\alpha$ for any median of $\rho$ and $\mu_\alpha=\sum_x\rho(x)x$ for its mean. The mean absolute deviation about $m_\alpha$ is $\mathrm{MAD}_{\mathrm m}(\rho)=\sum_x\rho(x)|x-m_\alpha|$, and its variance is $\mathrm{Var}(\rho)=\sum_x\rho(x)(x-\mu_\alpha)^2$.

\begin{corollary}[Median and mean consensus]\label{cor:mec-median-mean}
For every $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ and $\alpha\geq1$, the optimal consensus points for $\beta=1$ are precisely the medians of $\overline{\pi^\alpha}$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-median-case}
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,1}(\pi)
=\Sigma(\pi^\alpha)\,\mathrm{MAD}_{\mathrm m}(\overline{\pi^\alpha}).
\end{equation}
For $\beta=2$, the unique optimal consensus is $\mu_\alpha$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-mean-case}
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,2}(\pi)
=\Sigma(\pi^\alpha)\,\mathrm{Var}(\overline{\pi^\alpha}).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $G(y)=\sum_x\rho(x)|x-y|$. Its left and right derivatives are
\[
G'_-(y)=2\sum_{x<y}\rho(x)-1,
\qquad
G'_+(y)=2\sum_{x\leq y}\rho(x)-1.
\]
The convex minimum condition $G'_-(y)\leq0\leq G'_+(y)$ is equivalent to the two median inequalities. This proves the characterization for $\beta=1$, including nonunique minima. For $\beta=2$, expansion around the mean gives
\[
\sum_x\rho(x)(x-y)^2
=\mathrm{Var}(\rho)+(y-\mu_\alpha)^2.
\]
The second term is minimized uniquely at $\mu_\alpha$. Multiplication by $\Sigma(\pi^\alpha)$ yields both values.
\end{proof}

When $\alpha=1$, these are the median and mean of the original normalized distribution, whereas for general $\alpha$, they belong to its normalized power distribution. This distinction matters when computing the consensus point. The minimization of absolute-distance effort explains why mean absolute deviation about a median, rather than median absolute deviation, appears in the linear case.

\begin{example}[The influence of group weights]\label{ex:mec-weighted-consensus}
Consider the distributions from the paper
\[
\pi=((40,60),(0.25,0.75)),
\qquad
\pi'=((40,59,1),(0.25,0.75,1)).
\]
For $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=2$, their consensuses are $0.55$ and $0.5525$, respectively, and
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{1,2}(\pi)=6
<6.161875=\mathrm{MEC}_{1,2}(\pi').
\]
For $\alpha=\beta=2$, the transformed means are $31/52$ and $1721/2904$, and the ordering reverses,
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi)=\frac{3600}{13}
>\frac{3185183}{11616}=\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi').
\]
Transferring one unit of mass toward the extreme increases variance in the first case. In the second, the transformed weights of the two main groups give a lower minimum after the transfer. This example illustrates the role of $\alpha$; it does not establish a general rule for partial transfers.
\end{example}

The median characterization also gives a geometric interpretation of the linear case. It supplies the proof of the identity stated in Eq.~\eqref{eq:MEC-extremal-intro} without adding a new definition of polarization.

\begin{theorem}[Linear MEC and distance to the extremal distribution]\label{th:mec-linear-extremal}
For every $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ with total mass $M$,
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}(\pi)
=\frac{M}{2}-\mathrm{EMD}(\pi,\pi^\star).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $m$ be a median of $\pi$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:mec-consensus-transport} and Corollary~\ref{cor:mec-median-mean}, $\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}(\pi)=\mathrm{EMD}(\pi,\pi_m)$. For distributions of equal mass on the line, transportation distance has the quantile representation \citep{vallender1974calculation,geher2020isometric}
\[
\mathrm{EMD}(\rho,\eta)
=\int_0^M|Q_\rho(q)-Q_\eta(q)|\,dq,
\]
where, for a distribution $\rho$ of mass $M$ and $q\in[0,M]$,
\[
Q_\rho(q)=\max\left\{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\rho)
\,\middle|\,\sum_{z<x}\rho(z)\leq q\right\}.
\]
This quantile function records opinion positions in order of cumulative mass. Choices at the finitely many cumulative-mass boundaries do not affect the integral. Below $M/2$, $Q_{\pi^\star}=0$ and $Q_\pi\leq m$ almost everywhere. Above $M/2$, $Q_{\pi^\star}=1$ and $Q_\pi\geq m$ almost everywhere. Since $Q_{\pi_m}=m$, the distance separates pointwise into the two parts through $Q_\pi$, giving
\[
\mathrm{EMD}(\pi^\star,\pi_m)
=\mathrm{EMD}(\pi^\star,\pi)+\mathrm{EMD}(\pi,\pi_m).
\]
The left-hand side is $(M/2)m+(M/2)(1-m)=M/2$. Rearranging and using symmetry of EMD proves the claim.
\end{proof}

For example, $\pi=((20,30,50),(0,0.8,1))$ has a median at $0.8$. Its linear effort there is $20(0.8)+30(0)+50(0.2)=26$, so $\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}(\pi)=26$ and $\mathrm{EMD}(\pi,\pi^\star)=50-26=24$. The identity shows that linear MEC lies between zero and $M/2$, with the extremal distribution attaining the upper value. For the generalized family, the proof of maximality requires the subsequent analysis of movements of groups.

\subsection{Symmetry and Population Scaling}\label{subsec:mec-symmetry-scaling}

The dispersion characterization concerns the minimum within a distribution. We now examine transformations that preserve its value or change it by a known factor. These properties make the role of orientation, location, and population size precise.

\begin{definition}[Reflection, shift, and symmetry]\label{def:mec-reflections-shifts}
For $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$, a point $c\in[0,1]$ is an admissible reflection center if $2c-x\in[0,1]$ for every occupied $x$. The reflected distribution has masses $\pi^{\mathrm{ref}(c)}(2c-x)=\pi(x)$. The distribution is symmetric about $c$ if $\pi=\pi^{\mathrm{ref}(c)}$.

A shift $t\in\mathbb{R}$ is admissible if $x+t\in[0,1]$ for every occupied $x$. The shifted distribution has masses $\pi^{\mathrm{shift}(t)}(x+t)=\pi(x)$. Reflections and shifts have zero mass outside their transformed supports.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Reflection and shift invariance]\label{lem:mec-geometric-invariance}
For every $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ and $\alpha,\beta\geq1$, admissible reflections and shifts preserve MEC,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-geometric-invariance}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}(c)})
 &=\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi),\\
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{shift}(t)})
 &=\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:mec-existence}, minima can be taken over the interval spanned by the support. Reflection maps that interval bijectively onto the reflected interval. For a point $y$ in the former,
\[
\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi^{\mathrm{ref}(c)}}(2c-y)
=\sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)}\pi(x)^\alpha|(2c-x)-(2c-y)|^\beta
=\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(y).
\]
The admissibility condition places both intervals inside $[0,1]$, so taking minima proves reflection invariance. Similarly, a shift maps the support interval onto the shifted interval and satisfies $\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi^{\mathrm{shift}(t)}}(y+t)=\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(y)$. Taking minima gives shift invariance.
\end{proof}
The argument uses the intervals spanned by occupied opinions because an admissible transformation of the support need not map the whole interval $[0,1]$ onto itself. It also shows that optimal consensus points transform with the occupied positions. Reflection preserves the value while reversing orientation; a common shift preserves the value while changing the location of the distribution. Neither operation changes group masses or their mutual distances.

\begin{lemma}[Consensus at a symmetry center]\label{lem:mec-symmetry-center}
If $\pi$ is symmetric about $c$, then for every $\alpha,\beta\geq1$,
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi)
=\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(c).
\]
For $\beta>1$, $c$ is the unique optimal consensus point. For $\beta=1$, it is a median of $\overline{\pi^\alpha}$, though other medians may also be optimal.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The transformed masses of each symmetric pair are equal. If $\beta>1$, reflection invariance maps the unique optimum to another optimum, so $y^\star=2c-y^\star$, giving $y^\star=c$. If $\beta=1$, the transformed mass strictly to the left of $c$ equals the mass strictly to its right. Each is at most half the total transformed mass. Thus $c$ is a median, and Corollary~\ref{cor:mec-median-mean} gives the minimum there.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Effort of an extremal distribution]\label{cor:mec-extremal-value}
For every $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ with total mass $M$ and every $\alpha,\beta\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-extremal-value}
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi^\star)
=2\left(\frac{M}{2}\right)^\alpha\left(\frac12\right)^\beta
=2^{1-\alpha-\beta}M^\alpha.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The extremal distribution is symmetric about $1/2$. Lemma~\ref{lem:mec-symmetry-center} gives its minimum there, and the two groups each contribute $(M/2)^\alpha(1/2)^\beta$.
\end{proof}
When $\beta>1$, the extremal distribution has its unique optimal consensus at $1/2$. When $\beta=1$, its equally weighted endpoints make every point in $[0,1]$ a median of its normalized power distribution, so the effort is constant throughout the interval. The formula gives the value used as the reference for normalization in the proposal. Evaluating this profile and proving that it maximizes MEC over all distributions of the same mass are distinct steps. The latter belongs to the theoretical analysis that establishes the second requirement of Definition~\ref{def:mec-polarization-measure}.

\begin{theorem}[Population scaling]\label{th:mec-population-scaling}
For $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ and $\lambda>0$, define $(\lambda\pi)(x)=\lambda\pi(x)$. Then, for every $\alpha,\beta\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-population-scaling}
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda\pi)
=\lambda^\alpha\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi).
\end{equation}
Scaling all masses by $\lambda$ preserves the set of optimal consensus points.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For every candidate $y$, $\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\lambda\pi}(y)=\lambda^\alpha\mathrm{EC}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\pi}(y)$. The positive factor is independent of $y$, so it scales the minimum and leaves its minimizing positions unchanged.
\end{proof}

Two consequences specify how to compare populations. Applying Theorem~\ref{th:mec-population-scaling} with $\lambda=1/M$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-mass-normalization}
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\overline{\pi})
=\frac{\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi)}{M^\alpha}.
\end{equation}
For fixed parameters, multiplying the masses of two distributions by the same factor preserves their ordering,
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi)>\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi')
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda\pi)>
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda\pi').
\]
This is Condition~H of Esteban and Ray \citep{esteban1994measurement}, which concerns invariance of comparisons under a common scaling, rather than invariance of the raw score. Normalizing each population removes its own total-mass factor. Dividing instead by the extremal value in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mec-extremal-value} gives
\[
\frac{\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi)}
     {\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi^\star)}
=2^{\alpha+\beta-1}\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\overline{\pi}).
\]
For a fixed parameter pair, the two normalized quantities differ by a positive constant and induce the same ranking. Their numerical scales remain distinct.

These properties complete the construction needed for the next parts of the study. The effort has a global minimum, its optimal positions have precise characterizations, and its changes under reflection, common shifts, and population scaling are known. This gives a basis for proving how group movements, mergers, and splits affect MEC, and for computing and comparing its values under explicit numerical conventions.

\section{Theoretical Results and Axiomatic Analysis}\label{sec:mec-theory}

This section first establishes maximality at the balanced extreme distribution, then examines the three Esteban--Ray axioms and central-split monotonicity. The Minority Principle and the Tipping Point method complete the analysis by clarifying how the effort is shared and why a partial transfer toward an extreme may reduce polarization. The results and proof strategies follow the completed paper \citep{aranda2026mec}.

Throughout, $\alpha,\beta\geq1$, distributions belong to $\mathcal{D}$, and all opinion positions lie in $[0,1]$. When these parameters are fixed, we write $\mathrm{MEC}(\pi)$ and $\mathrm{EC}^{\pi}(u)$ for the corresponding minimum and effort. The notation for powers, optimal consensus, and extremal distributions remains that of Section~\ref{sec:mec-foundations}.

\subsection{Group Movements and Maximal Polarization}\label{subsec:mec-group-movements}

An increase in distance from an optimal consensus does not by itself establish an increase in MEC, because the amount of mass moved matters. Example~\ref{ex:mec-weighted-consensus} already showed that detaching a small fragment from a group can lower MEC, even when that fragment moves toward an extreme. We begin with the different operation in which an entire group moves.

\begin{definition}[Movements of groups]\label{def:mec-group-movement}
For $a,b\in[0,1]$ with $a\neq b$, let $\pi_{a\to b}$ be the distribution obtained by moving the whole mass at $a$ to $b$,
\[
\pi_{a\to b}(a)=0,\qquad
\pi_{a\to b}(b)=\pi(a)+\pi(b),\qquad
\pi_{a\to b}(x)=\pi(x)\quad(x\notin\{a,b\}).
\]
Set $\pi_{a\to a}=\pi$. The movement is a \emph{shift away from consensus} if, for every optimal consensus point $y^\star$ of $\pi$, either $b<a\leq y^\star$ or $y^\star\leq a<b$.

A movement is a \emph{median-shift} if $\beta=1$, $a$ is occupied and is the unique optimal consensus point of $\pi$, and $b\neq a$.
\end{definition}

The requirement concerning every optimum matters when $\beta=1$, since the median interval may contain several points. The definition also allows the moved group to merge with a group already at its destination, in which case its new weight is $(\pi(a)+\pi(b))^\alpha$, rather than the sum of their separate power weights.

\begin{theorem}[Shifting away from consensus]\label{th:mec-shifting-away}
Let $\pi$ have at least two occupied positions, and let $a\in\boldsymbol{s}(\pi)$. If $\pi_{a\to b}$ is a shift away from consensus and is not a median-shift, then
\[
\mathrm{MEC}(\pi_{a\to b})>\mathrm{MEC}(\pi).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First consider a distribution $\rho$ with linear mass weights, so the effort is $\mathrm{EC}_{1,\beta}^{\rho}$. By reflection, it is enough to treat a rightward shift $a<b$, with all original optima at or to the left of $a$.

For $\beta=1$, let $[l,r]$ be the median interval of $\rho$. If $l<r$, exactly half the mass lies strictly below $r$, and moving the mass at $a\geq r$ to $b$ leaves that amount unchanged. Thus $r$ remains a median. If $l=r$, exclusion of a median-shift gives $a>r$, so the masses on either side of $r$ and at $r$ are unchanged. Again, $r$ remains a median. Evaluation at this common optimum gives
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}(\rho_{a\to b})-
\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}(\rho)=\rho(a)(b-a)>0.
\]

For $\beta>1$, let $y^\star$ be the original optimum and write $w=\rho(a)>0$. Define $f(v)=\operatorname{sign}(v)|v|^{\beta-1}$, with $f(0)=0$, and
\[
H(u)=\sum_{x\neq a}\rho(x)f(u-x).
\]
There is positive mass outside $a$, so $H$ is strictly increasing. The original balance equation is $H(y^\star)+wf(y^\star-a)=0$. For $t>a$, the balance function after moving the group to $t$ is $H(u)+wf(u-t)$. It is negative at $y^\star$ and positive at $t$, because $t>a\geq y^\star$ and $H(y^\star)\geq0$. Its unique zero $y_t$ therefore satisfies $y^\star<y_t<t$.

The objective and its partial derivative with respect to $t$ are continuous for $\beta>1$, and the minimizer on the compact interval $[0,1]$ is unique. The envelope theorem therefore gives the derivative of the minimized effort $G(t)=\mathrm{MEC}_{1,\beta}(\rho_{a\to t})$,
\[
G'(t)=\beta w(t-y_t)^{\beta-1}>0\qquad(t>a).
\]
This argument does not require differentiability of the map $t\mapsto y_t$. Continuity at $t=a$ then gives $G(b)>G(a)$.

To pass to general $\alpha$, apply this result to $\rho=\pi^\alpha$. It has the same optimal consensus points as $\pi$ under the original parameters. If $b$ was already occupied, moving its power weights separately gives mass $\pi(a)^\alpha+\pi(b)^\alpha$ there, whereas merging the original masses and then taking powers gives $(\pi(a)+\pi(b))^\alpha$. Since
\[
\pi(a)^\alpha+\pi(b)^\alpha\leq(\pi(a)+\pi(b))^\alpha,
\]
the latter effort is pointwise at least as large. Taking minima yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi)
&<\mathrm{MEC}_{1,\beta}((\pi^\alpha)_{a\to b})\\
&\leq\mathrm{MEC}_{1,\beta}((\pi_{a\to b})^\alpha)
=\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi_{a\to b}).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

The non-consensus hypothesis is explicit here. Moving the only group of a consensus distribution produces another consensus and leaves MEC at zero. This is the boundary case implicit in the paper's argument, which begins with non-consensus distributions. The median-shift exception is different and can occur when several groups are present.

\begin{example}[A median-shift]\label{ex:mec-median-shift}
For $\alpha=\beta=1$, let
\[
\pi=((20,60,20),(0,0.5,1)),\qquad
\pi'=\pi_{0.5\to0.9}=((20,60,20),(0,0.9,1)).
\]
Their unique medians are $0.5$ and $0.9$, respectively. Their minimum efforts are $20(0.5)+20(0.5)=20$ and $20(0.9)+20(0.1)=20$. This shift away from the original consensus leaves MEC unchanged.
\end{example}

The movement theorem brings groups toward the endpoints. The next result determines which division of mass between those endpoints has the greatest minimum effort.

\begin{theorem}[Balanced two-point maximum]\label{th:mec-two-point-maximum}
Let $M>0$ and $A\in[0,M]$. For $\rho_A=((A,M-A),(0,1))$,
\[
\mathrm{MEC}(\rho_A)\leq\mathrm{MEC}(\rho_{M/2}).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The cases $A=0$ and $A=M$ are consensuses. For $0<A<M$ and $\beta=1$, the median formula gives
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,1}(\rho_A)=\min\{A^\alpha,(M-A)^\alpha\},
\]
which is greatest at $A=M/2$.

For $\beta>1$, the unique optimum $u_A\in(0,1)$ satisfies
\[
A^\alpha u_A^{\beta-1}=(M-A)^\alpha(1-u_A)^{\beta-1}.
\]
Writing $g(A)=\mathrm{MEC}(\rho_A)$, this equality gives both $g(A)=A^\alpha u_A^{\beta-1}$ and $g(A)=(M-A)^\alpha(1-u_A)^{\beta-1}$. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
g(A)^2
&=[A(M-A)]^\alpha[u_A(1-u_A)]^{\beta-1}\\
&\leq\left(\frac{M}{2}\right)^{2\alpha}
       \left(\frac14\right)^{\beta-1}
=g(M/2)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Both values are nonnegative, so the required inequality follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[MEC is a polarization measure]\label{th:mec-polarization-measure}
For every $\alpha,\beta\geq1$, MEC satisfies Definition~\ref{def:mec-polarization-measure}. In particular, for every $\pi\in\mathcal{D}$ with mass $M$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-general-maximum}
0\leq\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi)
\leq\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\pi^\star)
=2^{1-\alpha-\beta}M^\alpha.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Section~\ref{subsec:mec-convexity} established that MEC is zero at consensus. It remains to prove maximality. A consensus already satisfies the inequality, so assume at least two occupied positions.

For $\beta>1$, move an interior group to the endpoint on its side of the unique optimum. A group located at the optimum may move to either endpoint. Each operation is a shift away from consensus and strictly increases MEC by Theorem~\ref{th:mec-shifting-away}. It removes an interior occupied position without creating another one. After finitely many operations only the endpoints remain occupied. Theorem~\ref{th:mec-two-point-maximum} bounds this final value by that of $\pi^\star$.

For $\beta=1$, perform the same operations whenever they are not median-shifts. A nontrivial median interval permits moving its occupied endpoints outward. The process therefore ends either with support $\{0,1\}$ or with support contained in $\{0,m,1\}$, where an occupied interior $m$ is the unique median of the final power distribution. Write this final distribution as $\mu$, with total mass $M$.

In the latter case, $\mu(0),\mu(1)<M/2$. Indeed, if an endpoint mass were at least $M/2$, its power weight would be at least the sum of all other power weights, by superadditivity, so that endpoint would be a median. This would contradict uniqueness at $m$. Since $m$ is also an optimum for $\pi^\star$ when $\beta=1$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,1}(\pi)
&\leq\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,1}(\mu)
=\mu(0)^\alpha m+\mu(1)^\alpha(1-m)\\
&<\left(\frac{M}{2}\right)^\alpha m
 +\left(\frac{M}{2}\right)^\alpha(1-m)
=\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,1}(\pi^\star).
\end{aligned}
\]
An unoccupied endpoint contributes zero. If the process ends with both endpoints alone, Theorem~\ref{th:mec-two-point-maximum} applies instead. The extremal value is Eq.~\eqref{eq:mec-extremal-value}.
\end{proof}

This establishes the upper bound needed for the extremal normalization introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:mec-symmetry-scaling}. It holds throughout $\alpha,\beta\geq1$. Compliance with the Esteban--Ray axioms is a further question and has a different parameter restriction.

\subsection{Esteban--Ray Axioms}\label{subsec:mec-er-axioms}

Section~\ref{subsec:lit-axioms} explained identification and alienation and distinguished axiomatic compliance from characterization. We now state the axioms using the paper's formulations and prove the corresponding MEC results. I developed the ER-axiom and central-split proofs with Jesús Aranda's guidance; Frank Valencia reviewed and improved this material. Let $P\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}$ be a polarization measure. The conditions below are those used in the paper, following Esteban and Ray \citep{esteban1994measurement,aranda2026mec}. They concern specified configurations rather than arbitrary mergers or movements.

\begin{axiom}[ER Axiom 1, increased identification]\label{axiom:mec-er1}
For every $p>0$ and $x\in(0,1)$, there exist $\varepsilon,\mu>0$ such that, for every $x<y\leq1$ and $0<q<p$ satisfying $y-x<\varepsilon$ and $q<\mu p$,
\[
P((p,q,q),(0,x,y))
<P\left((p,2q),\left(0,\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\right).
\]
\end{axiom}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{images/axiom1.png}
\caption{The merger in ER Axiom~1, reproduced from the paper \citep{aranda2026mec}. Two nearby groups of mass $q$ merge at their midpoint, forming a group of mass $2q$.}
\label{fig:mec-er-identification}
\end{figure}

Two groups must be sufficiently small relative to the group at zero and sufficiently close to one another. Under those conditions, consolidating them at their midpoint must increase polarization. The existential bounds on proximity and relative mass are part of the axiom. Figure~\ref{fig:mec-er-identification} shows the merger.

\begin{theorem}[Identification threshold]\label{th:mec-er1}
For $\alpha,\beta\geq1$, MEC satisfies ER Axiom~1 if and only if $\alpha>1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix $p>0$ and $x\in(0,1)$. Write $h=y-x>0$, and let
\[
\pi=((p,q,q),(0,x,x+h)),\qquad
\widetilde\pi=((p,2q),(0,x+h/2)).
\]
Write their efforts as $E(u)$ and $\widetilde E(u)$. For $0\leq u<x$, set $A=x-u$. Their difference is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-identification-difference}
E(u)-\widetilde E(u)=q^\alpha B(A,h),
\end{equation}
where
\[
\begin{aligned}
B(A,h)
&=A^\beta+(A+h)^\beta-2^\alpha(A+h/2)^\beta\\
&=J(A,h)-(2^\alpha-2)(A+h/2)^\beta,\\
J(A,h)
&=A^\beta+(A+h)^\beta-2(A+h/2)^\beta.
\end{aligned}
\]
Convexity gives $J\geq0$. It is zero when $\beta=1$ and strictly positive when $\beta>1$ and $h>0$.

For $\beta=1$, choose $\mu=1/2$. Then $q<p/2$ makes zero the unique power-weighted median of both distributions, and
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,1}(\pi)=q^\alpha(2x+h),\qquad
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,1}(\widetilde\pi)
=2^{\alpha-1}q^\alpha(2x+h).
\]
The required strict inequality holds for $\alpha>1$. For $\alpha=1$, any proposed bounds $\varepsilon,\mu>0$ admit $0<q<\min\{p/2,\mu p\}$ and $0<h<\min\{\varepsilon,1-x\}$. These distributions have equal minimum efforts, so no such bounds can ensure the axiom's strict inequality.

Now assume $\beta>1$. Choose
\[
\mu_0=\min\left\{
(1+2^{\beta-1})^{-1/\alpha},
2^{-(\alpha+\beta-1)/\alpha}\right\}>0.
\]
For $q<\mu_0p$ and $0<h<x/2$, both derivatives are negative at zero and positive at $x/2$. In particular,
\[
\begin{aligned}
E'(x/2)&\geq\beta(x/2)^{\beta-1}
 [p^\alpha-(1+2^{\beta-1})q^\alpha]>0,\\
\widetilde E'(x/2)&\geq\beta(x/2)^{\beta-1}
 [p^\alpha-2^{\alpha+\beta-1}q^\alpha]>0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Strict convexity puts both optima in $(0,x/2)$.

If $\alpha>1$, then $B(A,0)=-(2^\alpha-2)A^\beta<0$. Continuity on the compact interval $A\in[x/2,x]$ gives $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that $B(A,h)<0$ throughout that interval whenever $0<h<\varepsilon_0$. Choose $\mu=\mu_0$ and $\varepsilon=\min\{\varepsilon_0,x/2\}$. At the optimum $\widetilde u$ of $\widetilde E$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:mec-identification-difference} yields
\[
\mathrm{MEC}(\pi)\leq E(\widetilde u)
<\widetilde E(\widetilde u)=\mathrm{MEC}(\widetilde\pi).
\]
Thus the axiom holds.

If $\alpha=1$, let any proposed $\varepsilon,\mu>0$ be given. Choose $0<h<\min\{\varepsilon,x/2,1-x\}$ and $0<q<\min\{\mu p,p/(1+2^{\beta-1})\}$. The optimum $u$ of $E$ lies in $(0,x/2)$ by the same derivative bounds. Here $B(A,h)=J(A,h)>0$, so
\[
\mathrm{MEC}(\widetilde\pi)\leq\widetilde E(u)
<E(u)=\mathrm{MEC}(\pi).
\]
The required inequality fails for arbitrarily close, arbitrarily small groups. This proves necessity.
\end{proof}

The gain in identification is represented by the factor $2^\alpha-2$, and at $\alpha=1$, that gain disappears. This explains why the linear transport and variance instances discussed earlier do not satisfy Axiom~1, despite satisfying the basic definition of a polarization measure.

\begin{axiom}[ER Axiom 2, strengthened movement condition]\label{axiom:mec-er2}
For all $p,q,r>0$ with $p>r$, all $0<x<y\leq1$ with $y-x<x$, and all $\Delta\in(0,y-x)$,
\[
P((p,q,r),(0,x,y))
<P((p,q,r),(0,x+\Delta,y)).
\]
\end{axiom}

Esteban and Ray originally require the increase only for sufficiently small positive movements \citep[p.~833]{esteban1994measurement}. The paper uses the stronger condition above, covering every $\Delta\in(0,y-x)$ \citep{aranda2026mec}. The proof below establishes this stronger result and hence the original axiom.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{images/axiom2.png}
\caption{The movement in ER Axiom~2, reproduced from the paper \citep{aranda2026mec}. The group of mass $q$ moves right, away from the larger group of mass $p$, under the conditions stated in the axiom.}
\label{fig:mec-er-alienation}
\end{figure}

The middle group moves toward the smaller right-hand group and away from the larger left-hand group. The conditions on distance and endpoint masses ensure that this increases alienation in the configuration specified by the axiom, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:mec-er-alienation}.

\begin{theorem}[Increased alienation]\label{th:mec-er2}
MEC satisfies ER Axiom~2 for all $\alpha,\beta\geq1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\pi=((p,q,r),(0,x,y))$ and $\pi_\Delta=\pi_{x\to x+\Delta}$. For $\beta>1$, the derivative of the original effort at $x$ is
\[
\beta\left[p^\alpha x^{\beta-1}-r^\alpha(y-x)^{\beta-1}\right]>0.
\]
Its unique optimum lies strictly left of $x$. Moving the whole middle group to $x+\Delta$ is therefore a shift away from consensus, and Theorem~\ref{th:mec-shifting-away} proves the claim.

For $\beta=1$, $p^\alpha>r^\alpha$ ensures that every median of $\pi^\alpha$ lies in $[0,x]$. If the movement is not a median-shift, the same theorem applies. Otherwise, $x$ is the unique median, and preserving the three power weights makes $x+\Delta$ the unique median after the movement. Evaluation at these two optima gives
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,1}(\pi_\Delta)
-\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,1}(\pi)
=(p^\alpha-r^\alpha)\Delta>0.
\]
\end{proof}

The median-shift excluded from the general movement theorem is thus harmless under the stronger asymmetry conditions of Axiom~2. These conditions supply the strict increase that a general median-shift need not have.

\begin{axiom}[ER Axiom 3, dispersion to extremes]\label{axiom:mec-er3}
For all $p,q>0$, all $0<x\leq1/2$, and all $\Delta\in(0,q/2)$,
\[
P((p,q,p),(0,x,2x))
<P((p+\Delta,q-2\Delta,p+\Delta),(0,x,2x)).
\]
\end{axiom}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{images/axiom3.png}
\caption{The redistribution in ER Axiom~3, reproduced from the paper \citep{aranda2026mec}. Mass $\Delta$ is transferred from the centre to each existing outer group; $y=2x$ in the symmetric configuration.}
\label{fig:mec-er-extremes}
\end{figure}

Equal portions of the central mass are transferred to the two existing outer groups. Their positions remain fixed, and positive mass remains at the centre. Figure~\ref{fig:mec-er-extremes} displays this redistribution.

\begin{theorem}[Dispersion to extremes]\label{th:mec-er3}
MEC satisfies ER Axiom~3 for all $\alpha,\beta\geq1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Both distributions are symmetric about $x$. Lemma~\ref{lem:mec-symmetry-center} makes $x$ an optimum for each, including $\beta=1$. Their minimum efforts are $2p^\alpha x^\beta$ and $2(p+\Delta)^\alpha x^\beta$. Since $\Delta>0$ and $x>0$, the latter is strictly greater.
\end{proof}

Together, these results establish compliance with all three original Esteban--Ray axioms exactly when $\alpha>1$ and $\beta\geq1$, within the standing parameter domain. They refine the proposal's hypothesis, which allowed $\alpha=1$ and restricted $\beta$ to values greater than one. They do not prove compliance with the strengthened KNS characterization discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:lit-axioms}, nor do they identify MEC with the pairwise ER function.

\subsection{Central-Split Monotonicity}\label{subsec:mec-central-split}

Axiom~3 sends central mass directly to existing outer groups. The additional property proposed in the research allows the moved portions to occupy intermediate positions instead. This supplies the central-split analysis envisaged in the proposal without imposing a five-category restriction on the mathematical result.

\begin{definition}[Central-split monotonicity, Property 1]\label{def:mec-central-split}
A polarization measure $P$ satisfies \emph{Property~1} if the following holds. Let $p\geq0$, $q>0$, $0<x\leq1/2$, $0<z\leq x$, and $0<\Delta<q/2$. Start from
\[
\pi=((p,q,p),(0,x,2x)),
\]
and move mass $\Delta$ from $x$ to each of $x-z$ and $x+z$, obtaining $\widetilde\pi$. Then $P(\widetilde\pi)>P(\pi)$.
\end{definition}

For $z<x$, the resulting representation is
\[
\widetilde\pi=((p,\Delta,q-2\Delta,\Delta,p),
               (0,x-z,x,x+z,2x)).
\]
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{images/property1.png}
\caption{Central splitting into distinct intermediate groups for $0<z<x$, reproduced from the paper \citep{aranda2026mec}. The new groups each have mass $\Delta$, while $q-2\Delta$ remains at the centre and the outer groups retain mass $p$. Here $y=2x$.}
\label{fig:mec-central-split}
\end{figure}

For $z=x$, the moved portions merge with the endpoint groups, giving the distribution in Axiom~3, now also allowing $p=0$. Figure~\ref{fig:mec-central-split} shows the intermediate-position case. The distinction prevents duplicated positions from being treated as separate groups. Zero outer masses are permitted, but the remaining central mass is positive.

\begin{theorem}[Central-split increase]\label{th:mec-central-split}
MEC satisfies Property~1 for all $\alpha,\beta\geq1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Both distributions are symmetric about $x$, which is an optimal consensus for each by Lemma~\ref{lem:mec-symmetry-center}. For $z<x$, evaluation there gives
\[
\mathrm{MEC}(\widetilde\pi)-\mathrm{MEC}(\pi)
=2\Delta^\alpha z^\beta>0.
\]
For $z=x$, merging at the endpoints instead gives
\[
\mathrm{MEC}(\widetilde\pi)-\mathrm{MEC}(\pi)
=2\big[(p+\Delta)^\alpha-p^\alpha\big]x^\beta>0.
\]
Both inequalities also hold when $p=0$.
\end{proof}

Property~1 is not implied by the three original ER axioms. The paper supplies a counterexample for $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}$ using
\[
\pi=((0.25,0.5,0.25),(0,0.5,1)),
\]
and the split $\Delta=z=0.1$, which gives
\[
\widetilde\pi=((0.25,0.1,0.3,0.1,0.25),(0,0.4,0.5,0.6,1)).
\]
Evaluation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lit-er} gives $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}(\pi)\approx0.154263K$ and $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}(\widetilde\pi)\approx0.122895K$. Thus the score decreases for every common normalization constant $K>0$, as in the paper's counterexample \citep{aranda2026mec}. This example distinguishes the added property from the original axioms; it does not establish that MEC exceeds ER under every criterion.

\subsection{Minority Principle}\label{subsec:mec-minority}

The previous results compare distributions before and after a transformation. We now examine how the groups within one distribution contribute to its minimum effort. In a distribution with two unequal groups, the optimal compromise lies closer to the larger group. The paper shows that the smaller group nevertheless contributes more of the total effort, throughout the parameter domain.

\begin{lemma}[Consensus and relative group size]\label{lem:mec-two-group-consensus}
Let $\pi=((A,B),(l,r))$ with $0\leq l<r\leq1$ and $A,B>0$. For $\beta>1$, its optimum $y^\star$ lies in $(l,r)$ and satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-two-group-balance}
A^\alpha(y^\star-l)^{\beta-1}
=B^\alpha(r-y^\star)^{\beta-1}.
\end{equation}
It lies to the right of $(l+r)/2$ if $B>A$, to the left if $A>B$, and at the midpoint if $A=B$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The derivative is negative at $l$ and positive at $r$. Strict convexity therefore gives an interior zero and Eq.~\eqref{eq:mec-two-group-balance}. At the midpoint, the derivative has the sign of $A^\alpha-B^\alpha$. Its strict increase locates the zero on the claimed side.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Minority Principle]\label{th:mec-minority}
Let $\pi=((A,B),(l,r))$ with $0\leq l<r\leq1$ and unequal positive masses $A,B$. At an optimal consensus $y^\star$, the smaller group contributes strictly more total effort. In particular,
\[
\begin{aligned}
A<B&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 A^\alpha(y^\star-l)^\beta>B^\alpha(r-y^\star)^\beta,\\
A>B&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 A^\alpha(y^\star-l)^\beta<B^\alpha(r-y^\star)^\beta.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Assume $A<B$. For $\beta>1$, Lemma~\ref{lem:mec-two-group-consensus} gives $y^\star-l>r-y^\star$. Multiplying the equal positive sides of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mec-two-group-balance} by these unequal distances gives the first strict inequality. If $\beta=1$, the unique power-weighted median is $r$. The larger group's contribution is zero, whereas the smaller group's contribution is $A^\alpha(r-l)>0$. Reflection gives the case $A>B$.
\end{proof}

For $\beta>1$, equal masses instead give equal contributions at the midpoint. The theorem concerns exactly two occupied positions and the aggregate contributions stipulated by the model. It does not establish a corresponding rule for distributions with more groups or prescribe how a population should reach agreement. Its significance is that changing $\alpha$ and $\beta$ does not reverse the ordering of those two contributions when the masses are unequal.

\subsection{Tipping Point and Partial Mass Transfers}\label{subsec:mec-tipping}

The Minority Principle describes how effort is shared within a fixed two-group distribution. We now examine how the minimum changes when a portion of a group moves. Moving a whole group preserves its mass until any merger at the destination. Moving a proper portion to an unoccupied position reduces the original group and creates a new one. For $\alpha>1$, splitting a mass into smaller masses reduces the sum of their separate power weights. This is why the whole-group theorem cannot be applied to arbitrary partial transfers. The paper's Tipping Point method makes that distinction precise for $\alpha=\beta=2$.

For $a\neq b$ and $0\leq k\leq\pi(a)$, write $\pi_{a:k\to b}$ for the distribution with masses $\pi(a)-k$ at $a$ and $\pi(b)+k$ at $b$, unchanged elsewhere. This notation includes the original distribution at $k=0$ and the whole-group movement at $k=\pi(a)$.

The quadratic dispersion identity from Section~\ref{subsec:mec-dispersion} gives a useful pairwise formula. For nonnegative masses $p_i$ at distinct positions $x_i$, with $W=\sum_i p_i^2>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-quadratic-pairs}
\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi)
=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{i<j}p_i^2p_j^2(x_i-x_j)^2.
\end{equation}
Indeed, expansion of the right-hand numerator gives $W\sum_i p_i^2x_i^2-(\sum_i p_i^2x_i)^2$, which is $W$ times the minimum quadratic effort. This identity avoids solving another optimization problem for each transfer.

\begin{proposition}[Two-point transfer formulas]\label{prop:mec-tipping-formulas}
Let $m,n>0$ and $0\leq l<r<r'\leq1$. Set
\[
\pi=((m,n),(l,r)),\qquad
\pi^{(k)}=\pi_{r:k\to r'}=((m,n-k,k),(l,r,r')),
\]
for $k\in[0,n]$. Write $d=r-l$, $e=r'-r$, and $C=m^2+n^2$. Then
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi)=\frac{m^2n^2d^2}{C},
\]
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-tipping-transferred-value}
\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi^{(k)})
=\frac{m^2(n-k)^2d^2+m^2k^2(d+e)^2+(n-k)^2k^2e^2}
       {2k^2-2nk+C}.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-tipping-change}
\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi^{(k)})-\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi)
=\frac{kR_{m,n,d,e}(k)}{Q_{m,n}(k)},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mec-tipping-polynomial}
\begin{aligned}
R_{m,n,d,e}(k)
={}&e^2Ck^3-2e^2nCk^2\\
 &+\bigl(2d^2m^4+2de\,m^2C+e^2C^2\bigr)k-2d^2m^4n,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and $Q_{m,n}(k)=C(2k^2-2nk+C)>0$ on $[0,n]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:mec-quadratic-pairs} first to the two original groups, then to the three transferred masses. The power-weight sum after the transfer is $m^2+(n-k)^2+k^2=2k^2-2nk+C$, giving Eq.~\eqref{eq:mec-tipping-transferred-value}. Subtracting the original value over the common denominator $Q_{m,n}$ and expanding gives Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mec-tipping-change} and~\eqref{eq:mec-tipping-polynomial}. Finally,
\[
2k^2-2nk+C=2(k-n/2)^2+m^2+n^2/2>0.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Unique tipping point]\label{th:mec-tipping-point}
Under the assumptions of Proposition~\ref{prop:mec-tipping-formulas}, there is exactly one $k^\star\in(0,n)$ at which $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi^{(k^\star)})=\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi)$. Moreover,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi^{(k)})&<\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi)
 &&\text{for }0<k<k^\star,\\
\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi^{(k)})&>\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi)
 &&\text{for }k^\star<k\leq n.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The endpoint values of the cubic are
\[
R_{m,n,d,e}(0)=-2d^2m^4n<0,\qquad
R_{m,n,d,e}(n)=e(2d+e)m^2nC>0.
\]
They establish existence of a root in $(0,n)$. The discriminant argument in Appendix~\ref{app:mec-tipping-uniqueness}, adapted from the paper's Appendix~F, proves that this cubic has exactly one real root and that it is simple. Its sign is therefore negative below $k^\star$ and positive above it. The positive denominator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mec-tipping-change} proves the claims for $k>0$.
\end{proof}

The threshold is the transferred mass at which the original score is recovered, but it is not a stationary point of the transferred score. Below it, the new score is smaller than the original; this does not assert that the score decreases throughout that interval as $k$ grows. Reflection gives the corresponding result for a transfer from the left-hand group to a position farther left.

\begin{example}[Critical transferred mass]\label{ex:mec-tipping-point}
For the distribution already used in Example~\ref{ex:mec-weighted-consensus}, take $m=40$, $n=60$, $l=0.25$, $r=0.75$, and $r'=1$. Then $d=1/2$, $e=1/4$, and
\[
R_{40,60,1/2,1/4}(k)
=325k^3-39000k^2+5050000k-76800000.
\]
Its unique root in $(0,60)$ is $k^\star\approx17.156$. Thus moving one unit of mass reduces MEC, as previously observed, whereas transfers larger than approximately $17.156$ increase it above the original value. Equality holds at the threshold. The formula allows real-valued masses; integer respondent counts restrict the feasible transfers accordingly.
\end{example}

The cubic representation extends to a general finite support, but uniqueness of the crossing does not follow merely from that representation.

\begin{proposition}[Transfer from the maximal opinion to the extreme]\label{prop:mec-general-transfer}
Let $\pi=((p_1,\ldots,p_r),(x_1,\ldots,x_r))$ with positive masses and $0\leq x_1<\cdots<x_r<1$. For $k\in[0,p_r]$, let $\pi^{(k)}=\pi_{x_r:k\to1}$. There are polynomials $P_\pi$ and $Q_\pi$ such that
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi^{(k)})-\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi)
=\frac{kP_\pi(k)}{Q_\pi(k)},
\]
where $P_\pi$ is cubic and $Q_\pi(k)>0$ on $[0,p_r]$. For $k>0$, the sign of the change is the sign of $P_\pi(k)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Set $w_i=p_i^2$, $D=\sum_i w_i$, and $N=\sum_{i<j}w_iw_j(x_i-x_j)^2$. After adding the target position $x_{r+1}=1$, define
\[
w_i(k)=p_i^2\ (i<r),\qquad
w_r(k)=(p_r-k)^2,\qquad w_{r+1}(k)=k^2,
\]
and let $D(k)=\sum_iw_i(k)$ and $N(k)=\sum_{i<j}w_i(k)w_j(k)(x_i-x_j)^2$. Equation~\eqref{eq:mec-quadratic-pairs} gives
\[
\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi^{(k)})-\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}(\pi)
=\frac{DN(k)-ND(k)}{DD(k)}.
\]
The numerator has degree at most four and vanishes at $k=0$, so it equals $kP_\pi(k)$ for a polynomial of degree at most three. The coefficient of $k^4$ is $D(1-x_r)^2>0$, supplied by the pair joining $x_r$ and $1$. Thus $P_\pi$ is genuinely cubic. Finally,
\[
D(k)\geq(p_r-k)^2+k^2\geq p_r^2/2>0,
\]
so $Q_\pi(k)=DD(k)$ is positive.
\end{proof}

The theoretical analysis now answers the formal part of the research question under explicit conditions. MEC has the balanced extreme maximum for all standing parameters; its joint ER compliance requires $\alpha>1$; and its central-split property also holds at $\alpha=1$. The two applications give further information about the model's effort and its response to partial transfers. Their claims remain restricted to the configurations and parameters proved above. The next stage concerns how these results are implemented and how MEC's rankings compare with alternative measures and published expert judgments.

\section{Computational Methodology and Implementation}\label{sec:methods}

The preceding sections establish what MEC measures and how it behaves under specified transformations. We now explain how it is computed and compared with other indices. The empirical design follows Section~8 of the completed paper \citep{aranda2026mec}. It combines an exhaustive comparison on five-point histograms with a comparison against published expert ratings. I carried out these experiments within the joint research, with experimental proposals and guidance from Frank Valencia, Jesús Aranda, and Juan Francisco Díaz. The implementation and numerical conventions are documented through the \texttt{pol\_measures} repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ulvenforst/pol_measures}}

The approved proposal supplies the objectives for the corpus, software, comparisons, and reproducibility. The completed paper supplies the scientific protocol and reference parametrization, while the code provides the operational detail needed to interpret those comparisons. These sources have different roles. A planned procedure is not evidence that it was executed, and a script in the current repository does not by itself identify the version that produced a published figure. We therefore distinguish the paper's comparisons, the conventions of the available scripts, and the supplementary analyses supported by saved outputs.

\subsection{Numerical Computation of MEC}\label{subsec:methods-computation}

The mathematical definition permits arbitrary positive total mass, whereas the main empirical comparisons use proportions on a fixed scale. For a histogram $h=(h_1,\ldots,h_5)$ with total $N>0$, its numerical representation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:methods-histogram-representation}
x_i=\frac{i-1}{4},\qquad p_i=\frac{h_i}{N},\qquad i=1,\ldots,5.
\end{equation}
All five coordinates are retained, including categories with zero mass. Removing empty endpoint categories and rescaling the remaining coordinates would change the distances and hence the question being measured. The positions $1,\ldots,5$ used in the expert-data scripts are mapped to the same coordinates by min--max normalization.

This mass convention is compatible with the paper's count-based corpus. By Theorem~\ref{th:mec-population-scaling}, using counts rather than proportions multiplies MEC by $N^\alpha$. Since the exhaustive corpus fixes $N=100$, the multiplier is common to every histogram at a fixed $\alpha$. Its ranking is therefore unchanged. For profiles with different totals, each must be normalized by its own total when the comparison concerns opinion structure independently of population size.

The public \texttt{MEC} class precomputes $p_i^\alpha$ and passes the effort as a scalar objective to SciPy's \texttt{minimize\_\allowbreak scalar}, with \texttt{method='bounded'} and bounds $(0,1)$.\footnote{\url{https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.optimize.minimize_scalar.html}} It returns the computed minimum value, but the class does not return the optimizer's consensus point or termination record. A numerical value from this routine approximates the mathematical minimum; in particular, a consensus at an endpoint need not be returned as exactly zero.

For $\beta>1$, strict convexity justifies minimizing this objective without a search over competing local minima. The repository also provides \texttt{mec\_\allowbreak bisection\_\allowbreak value}, a reference solver based on the increasing balance function of Section~\ref{subsec:mec-convexity}. It brackets the optimum between the first and last supplied coordinates and repeatedly retains the half containing the derivative's zero. After $r$ iterations, the interval has width $(x_5-x_1)/2^r$. Thus
\[
r=\left\lceil\log_2\frac{x_5-x_1}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil
\]
iterations suffice when $x_5-x_1>\varepsilon$. The effort is evaluated at the final midpoint. With $s$ supplied categories, each balance evaluation costs $O(s)$, giving $O(s\log(1/\varepsilon))$ arithmetic work on a fixed bounded scale. This is the complexity of the bisection procedure, not a measured runtime guarantee for every implementation.

The helper defaults to $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$. Its tests also use $10^{-10}$ for the bracket width and compare the returned values with the SciPy implementation under explicit absolute and relative tolerances. These are separate criteria, since a bound on the consensus interval is not the same as a prescribed error in the effort value. The public SciPy implementation passes no explicit tolerance, so its settings are inherited from the installed SciPy version. The proposal's planned tolerances $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-8}$ cannot therefore be attributed to every historical call.

At $\beta=1$, the differentiable balance equation is unavailable and the bisection helper rejects the parameter. The weighted-median characterization in Corollary~\ref{cor:mec-median-mean} supplies an exact reference value. At $\beta=2$, the weighted-mean formula supplies another check. The public class nevertheless uses the bounded scalar optimizer for these cases as well. Analytical identities and numerical routes are kept distinct so that agreement between solvers can be tested against known values.

Mass normalization must also be distinguished from normalization of the index. For a unit-mass distribution $\bar\pi$ on the full scale, the maximality result of Section~\ref{subsec:mec-group-movements} gives the reference value $2^{1-\alpha-\beta}$. The class \texttt{MECNormalized} returns
\begin{equation}\label{eq:methods-root-normalization}
\left(\frac{\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\bar\pi)}
{\mathrm{MEC}_{\alpha,\beta}(\bar\pi^\star)}\right)^{1/\beta}.
\end{equation}
It evaluates the denominator numerically at the supplied endpoints with masses $(1/2,1/2)$. On the fixed full scale and within $\alpha,\beta\geq1$, this is a normalization to $[0,1]$, apart from numerical error. It differs from the direct ratio by a positive power. Both preserve rankings at fixed parameters before rounding. The normalized class must receive unit-mass weights for this interpretation; calling it on raw counts does not automatically supply the missing mass normalization.

\subsection{Library Implementation and Verification}\label{subsec:methods-software}

To apply these numerical conventions through a common interface, the repository separates numerical measures from input handling and experiment scripts. The base classes in \path{src/measures/base.py} define the callable interface and parameter access. Input handling is in \path{validation.py}; the measure implementations are divided between \path{metrics/proposed/} and \path{metrics/literature/}; the bisection reference is in \path{utils/optimization.py}. The \path{benchmarks/} directory contains the experiments, while \path{tests/} contains automated checks. This structure allows several measures to receive the same histogram without duplicating the comparison protocol.

The callable interface validates the supplied arrays and, by default, normalizes positions but preserves masses. Weight normalization requires \texttt{normalize\_\allowbreak weights=True}. Direct calls to \texttt{compute} bypass that histogram-processing step. The experiments must therefore either use the callable interface with the intended options or prepare the coordinates and weights explicitly before calling \texttt{compute}. A one-position mathematical consensus is represented in the five-category experiments by one positive weight and four zeros; the public histogram interface requires at least two supplied coordinates.

The interface can also assign categorical labels using the fixed cutoffs in \path{thresholds.py}, stored under percentile and $k$-means schemes for specified measures and parameter sets. It looks up these cutoffs rather than fitting them to the input histogram. Section~\ref{subsec:methods-thresholds} describes the historical threshold analysis associated with A12 and distinguishes its documented results from the current lookup procedure. The paper's ranking comparisons use numerical scores without these labels.

The package's parameter validator accepts positive exponents, including values below one. This software domain is broader than the theoretical domain established in the thesis. The computational searches described below may explore it, but the axiomatic results are invoked only under their stated conditions. In particular, a successful numerical evaluation does not imply convexity or compliance with the polarization axioms outside those conditions.

Verification includes input-validation tests, standard MEC profiles, normalization and scaling checks, and comparisons between the bounded optimizer and bisection. The bisection tests include nonuniform coordinates, several parameter pairs, and a deterministic random sweep with seed $20260528$. The tests are evidence about the implementation on the cases examined, and they complement the proofs rather than establish their universal claims.

The project provides a Python API, installation instructions, docstrings, and the test runner \texttt{python -m tests.run\_tests}. Its GitHub Actions workflow installs dependencies and runs that suite on pushes and pull requests. A configured workflow is not evidence that every remote run or declared Python version succeeds. The repository contains a GPLv3 license, and its package metadata identifies the distribution as \texttt{measures}, version \texttt{0.1.0a2}. The research repository retains the name \texttt{pol\_measures}.

Some experiment scripts expose command-line arguments. The package metadata does not define an installed \texttt{console\_\allowbreak scripts} entry point. These script interfaces are distinguished from the general library CLI promised in the proposal. Similarly, the available workflow establishes continuous-integration configuration, not an automated release or deployment pipeline. Their status remains part of the assessment of the software objective in Section~\ref{sec:objectives}.

\subsection{Data Sources and Computational Corpus}\label{sec:data}\label{subsec:methods-data}

With the numerical conventions and software interface specified, the first evaluation uses an exhaustive synthetic corpus. Following the paper, let
\[
\mathcal{D}_{100}^{(5)}=
\left\{h\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}^{5}\ \middle|\ \sum_{i=1}^{5}h_i=100\right\}.
\]
The stars-and-bars count is $\binom{104}{4}=4{,}598{,}126$. The comparison generator enumerates category allocations, forms their histograms, converts counts into proportions, and retains the lexicographically smaller of each histogram and its reversal. This implements the reflection reduction justified by the symmetry of the candidate measures \citep{aranda2026mec}.

Palindromic histograms are retained once. If $\mathscr{S}$ denotes this subset, its count follows from $2(h_1+h_2)+h_3=100$,
\[
|\mathscr{S}|=\sum_{j=0}^{50}(j+1)=1{,}326.
\]
The number of canonical representatives is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:methods-canonical-count}
\left|\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{100}^{(5)}\right|
=\frac{4{,}598{,}126+1{,}326}{2}
=2{,}299{,}726.
\end{equation}
This is the exact count reported in the completed paper. It replaces the proposal's approximation obtained by halving the full count without retaining the additional palindromes. The generator's separate counting function returns the full count, so progress totals must not be mistaken for the number of retained representatives.

Each canonical histogram contributes once to the comparison corpus. The reduction preserves the score assigned to a histogram and its reflection. It does not, by itself, assert equality between a correlation computed on this corpus and one computed on the full corpus with different multiplicities. The unit of analysis is the canonical profile. Exhaustive enumeration removes sampling uncertainty within this finite universe; it does not establish behavior on every distribution in $\mathcal{D}$.

The second corpus consists of the fifteen hypothetical five-point profiles and published expert scores used by Koudenburg, Kiers, and Kashima \citep{koudenburg2021new,aranda2026mec}. Their study collected ratings from sixty experts on a zero-to-one-hundred scale; the quantitative analysis retained fifty-eight after screening, as described in Section~\ref{subsec:lit-experts}. The thesis reuses the published benchmark and does not recruit experts or collect new judgments. The repository's \texttt{ValidationData} stores the histogram vectors, their identifiers, and the aggregate expert scores. Every profile is normalized by its own recorded total, which matters because the stored rows do not all sum to one hundred.

The aggregate ratings supply the reference ordering. They are distinct from KI, the fitted index implemented by the class \texttt{Experts}. Agreement with that formula and agreement with the published human scores are different comparisons. The available benchmark is not a file of individual expert responses or pairwise choices. A ranking induced from aggregate scores does not supply the participant-level evidence required to reconstruct a human paired-comparison study.

The proposal also includes regional illustrations using aggregate opinions from Valle del Cauca. Their role is descriptive. The companion manuscript documents a youth demonstration in August 2024 and a Cali City Council exercise in late 2024, with question wording, histograms, and comparisons between MEC and ER \citep[Section~12]{arandaMECcompanion}. These observations are separate from the synthetic and expert-data modules. Section~\ref{subsec:results-regional} summarizes the documented comparisons without presenting them as population estimates or as newly collected data.

The second applies the measures to the fifteen benchmark profiles and compares their scores with the published expert ratings. The implementation names do not always coincide with the paper's labels. In particular, \texttt{ShannonPol} computes the dissention expression of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lit-twd}, with machine epsilon added inside the logarithm. It is not the entropy $-\sum_i p_i\log_2p_i$. The class \texttt{Experts} divides the fitted zero-to-one-hundred scale by one hundred. The default ER constant normalizes the balanced extreme profile to one for unit mass. These positive rescalings preserve rankings before quantization. The \texttt{EMDPol} implementation uses category indices rather than arbitrary supplied distances, so its use here is restricted to the fixed equally spaced scale. Its geometric interpretation follows Section~\ref{subsec:mec-dispersion}.

The historical comparison scripts also include BiPol and additional parameter settings. Their presence does not enlarge the set of claims made in the completed paper. Likewise, a supplementary script comparing an optimized MEC variant must identify that variant separately from the paper's reference $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,1.15}$.

The primary agreement statistic is Kendall's $\tau_B$. Let $C$ and $D$ count concordant and discordant pairs, and let $T_1$ and $T_2$ count pairs tied only in the first and second score arrays, respectively. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:methods-kendall}
\tau_B=\frac{C-D}{\sqrt{(C+D+T_1)(C+D+T_2)}}.
\end{equation}
Pairs tied in both arrays contribute to none of these counts. A zero denominator gives an undefined statistic, not perfect agreement. The implementation uses \texttt{scipy.stats.kendalltau}, whose default is variant B.\footnote{\url{https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.kendalltau.html}} The statistic is applied directly to aligned score arrays. It is invariant under strictly increasing transformations that preserve ties, which matches the ordinal use of polarization measures in the paper. Pearson's correlation does not have this invariance and is not substituted for the primary criterion.

Numerical preprocessing is part of the protocol. The historical corpus calculator rounds each score to the nearest multiple of $10^{-4}$. The original expert-validation script truncates nonnegative scores to four decimal places, whereas the parameter-search scripts retain the computed floating-point values. These operations can introduce different ties and hence different values of $\tau_B$. The quantization step is not a solver tolerance. A figure or table must identify which convention generated its scores.

A supplementary optimized-validation report constructs strict permutations by stable sorting, which resolves tied values by their stored profile order. Such a strict-order comparison is not interchangeable with the score-based $\tau_B$ protocol when ties occur. The paper's tie-aware comparison is retained here; supplementary reports must disclose any different treatment.

For the reference parametrization, the paper reports that no evaluated pair strictly exceeded $(2,1.15)$ in Kendall concordance with the expert benchmark and that several pairs attained the same value \citep{aranda2026mec}. The available search pipeline documents a coarse grid followed by refinement and stability analysis. Its saved settings are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:methods-search}. This is an exhaustive evaluation of specified finite grids, not an optimization proof over the continuous parameter space.

The additional methods envisaged in the proposal are assessed under the scope criteria of Section~\ref{sec:objectives}. A shortlist of parameter candidates is distinct from top-$k$ overlap between profile rankings, and the saved grid does not establish preregistration.

\subsection{Threshold Classification}\label{subsec:methods-thresholds}

Ranking profiles does not assign them to levels of polarization. The proposal's desirable activity A12 addressed this further task through percentiles and $k$-means. The companion manuscript reports the analysis for $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,1.15}$ and $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}$ \citep[Section~9.4, pp.~29--32]{arandaMECthresholds}. My accompanying report also includes BiPol and supplies the tables to four decimal places \citep[``Threshold Analysis for Polarization Classification'']{narvaez2025thresholds}. The reported category counts sum to the $2{,}299{,}726$ canonical profiles of Eq.~\eqref{eq:methods-canonical-count}. Each profile contributes once, so these frequencies describe the synthetic corpus rather than the prevalence of polarization in a survey population.

BiPol is included here to retain the accompanying report's comparison and the three rows of its figures. For unit-mass inputs, the repository implementation divides occupied opinions at their weighted mean $\mu$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:lit-probabilities}. Let $p_L$ and $p_R$ be the total probabilities at positions below $\mu$ and at or above $\mu$, respectively, and let $\mu_L$ and $\mu_R$ be their conditional means. For a non-consensus profile, both groups have positive mass, and the implemented score is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:methods-bipol}
\mathrm{BiPol}(\overline\pi)=4p_Lp_R(\mu_R-\mu_L).
\end{equation}
At consensus, the implementation returns zero. This specifies the comparator used by the code without extending the paper's axiomatic or expert-validation claims to it.\footnote{The implementation is \path{src/measures/metrics/proposed/bipol.py}; the repository's README credits it to Carlos Pinzón.}

The percentile schemes use the 33rd and 66th percentiles for three categories, the 25th, 50th, and 75th for four, and the 20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th for five. They partition the ordered scores into groups of approximately prescribed sizes. The alternative applies one-dimensional $k$-means separately to each measure's scores, with $k=3,4,5$, to reduce the within-cluster sum of squares. Percentile boundaries depend on cumulative frequencies, whereas nearest-centroid boundaries lie halfway between adjacent ordered centroids. These criteria need not produce the same groups, and neither determines an optimal number of categories.

For Lloyd's procedure, let $z_r$ denote the score of canonical profile $r$, and let $c_j^{(t)}$ be centroid $j$ at iteration $t$. Starting from $k$ initial centroids, assign each profile to one nearest centroid,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:methods-lloyd-assignment}
j_r^{(t)}\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{1\leq j\leq k}
       (z_r-c_j^{(t)})^2,
\qquad
S_j^{(t)}=\{r:j_r^{(t)}=j\}.
\end{equation}
An equidistant profile must be assigned exclusively to one group; the report does not identify the historical tie-breaking rule. For each nonempty group, update its centroid to its mean,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:methods-lloyd-update}
c_j^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{|S_j^{(t)}|}\sum_{r\in S_j^{(t)}}z_r.
\end{equation}
Alternating these steps cannot increase $\sum_j\sum_{r\in S_j}(z_r-c_j)^2$, but does not guarantee a global minimum. These equations describe the reported procedure, not a recovered record of its initialization or stopping settings.

The historical tables report scores in $[0,1]$. The corpus generator and measure calculator are available, and the \texttt{MECNormalized} class already applied the root normalization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:methods-root-normalization} in repository commit \texttt{2970e547} of 3 March 2025. That calculator retains both raw and normalized MEC scores. The fitting step that selected a score array and produced the reported thresholds and figures has not been located, so its exact normalization and quantization cannot be established from that code alone. A nonlinear increasing transformation preserves order but can change a $k$-means fit. The results below therefore retain the sources' numerical scale. The stored cutoffs agree with the four-decimal report and are present in that historical commit. Their application is implemented and tested, while complete fitting arrays, initialization, restarts, and stopping settings remain unavailable among the inspected materials.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Computational Resources}\label{subsec:methods-reproducibility}

These distinctions determine what is needed to reproduce a comparison. The inputs must specify the fixed coordinates, count or proportion convention, and profile identifiers. The configuration must identify candidate implementations, parameters, normalization, solver route, and any rounding or truncation. The resulting score arrays, correlation matrices, and parameter tables must retain that configuration. Reproducing a rendered figure alone cannot resolve a difference between two numerical protocols.

The corpus comparison is organized in \path{benchmarks/comparison_matrix/}, while the published expert-data comparison and parameter searches are in \path{benchmarks/expert_validation/}. The saved search outputs include coarse and refined score tables, maxima, shortlisted candidates, stability summaries, and heatmaps. These artifacts document the supplementary settings in Table~\ref{tab:methods-search}. Associating them with a particular paper figure still requires matching the candidate set and numerical conventions.

The repository also contains timing experiments for the finite corpus and for increasing support size. They distinguish the public SciPy implementation from the bisection reference and record settings such as solver tolerance, workload, and execution environment. The support-size benchmark uses random normalized masses with seed $20260528$. Timing comparisons require the same inputs and numerical task, and their observed performance is reported separately from the mathematical operation count. The proposal's workstation specification is a planned resource declaration, not evidence that every saved run used that machine.

Enumeration and fixed-profile scoring are deterministic given the implementation and configuration. Seeds matter for the random support benchmarks and resampling analyses. Dependency versions also matter because the public optimizer inherits library defaults. A reproducible execution therefore records the repository revision, Python and scientific-library versions, platform, and experiment settings. The available timing metadata records several of these items; it does not establish that every historical output contains a complete revision record.



\section{Experimental Results}\label{sec:results}

The empirical evaluation asks how MEC ranks opinion distributions and how those rankings compare with the candidate measures and published expert judgments. The two main comparisons follow the completed paper \citep{aranda2026mec}. Their inputs and numerical conventions were specified in Section~\ref{sec:methods}. We first report the exhaustive comparison on five-point histograms, then the expert benchmark. Saved parameter-search outputs from \texttt{pol\_measures} provide supplementary evidence about sensitivity. Historical threshold results then examine how numerical scores were divided into categories, before the timing records address computational performance.

These comparisons serve different purposes. Agreement between indices describes how similarly they order distributions, while agreement with the published human scores assesses correspondence with expert judgments on the evaluated profiles. Neither replaces the axiomatic results established in Section~\ref{sec:mec-theory}.

Figure~\ref{fig:results-corpus-concordance} reproduces the full correlation matrix displayed in the paper. Each entry is Kendall's $\tau_B$ between two score arrays on that corpus. EMD occupies a single row because its mathematical score coincides with $\mathrm{MEC}_{1,1}$ on this scale.

Among the alternative families, the reference $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,1.15}$ agrees most closely with VdE ($0.744$), followed by EMD ($0.732$). Its concordance with $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}$ is $0.626$, with TWD $0.604$, and with KI $0.496$. These values locate MEC among related but distinct ways of ordering opinion profiles, but they do not identify a single comparator whose ranking MEC reproduces throughout the corpus.

The strongest off-diagonal entry is the agreement between TWD and $\mathrm{MEC}_{1,2}$, with $\tau_B=0.916$. The variance identity proved in Section~\ref{subsec:mec-dispersion} helps explain this result. On unit-mass profiles, $\mathrm{MEC}_{1,2}$ is variance, while TWD combines category masses with their distances from the mean. The close agreement is consistent with their shared emphasis on dispersion around the mean. It does not establish equality between the two functions.

The ER parameter also changes the empirical comparison substantially. At $0.8$, ER agrees most closely with $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,2}$ ($0.817$) and TWD ($0.784$). At $1.6$, its concordance with every other listed measure is below $0.4$, except with $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}$ itself ($0.553$). This pattern is consistent with the increased weight assigned to group identification by the larger ER exponent \citep{aranda2026mec}. It also shows why a result for one parametrization cannot be attributed to the whole ER family.

KI's largest off-diagonal entry is its concordance with TWD ($0.697$). Moderate agreement does not remove the limitation identified in the paper. Its formula assigns zero whenever one side of the scale is empty. For example, the unit-mass profile $(0,0,0.1,0.4,0.5)$ has KI equal to zero despite occupying three positions. MEC is positive on this profile by its characterization of consensus. This is a concrete difference in what the indices measure, rather than a conclusion drawn from the correlation matrix alone.

The matrix thus fulfills the proposal's principal comparison of rankings over the finite synthetic universe and is reproduced here as a published result. The present cross-check verified the candidate implementations and corpus count; it did not regenerate all the millions of score values underlying this matrix. Exhaustiveness refers to the paper's specified corpus, not to every finite opinion distribution or every parameter pair.

At the level of individual shapes, the experts assign the lowest score to the centrally concentrated profile~3 and the highest to the opposed-endpoint profile~14. Agreement is not complete, as the six discordant pairs show, but these profiles illustrate the distinction between widespread disagreement and opposed camps that motivated the proposal.

This evidence supports agreement on the evaluated benchmark. The same profiles informed MEC's reference parameter choice, and the benchmark is small and deliberately constructed. The reported concordance therefore does not establish performance on unseen profiles, on other scale lengths, or on regional survey populations. Nor does it measure agreement separately for the individual experts, whose responses are absent from the stored aggregate data.

\subsection{Regional Illustrations}\label{subsec:results-regional}

The companion manuscript reports two uses of the Polarizometer application within PROMUEVA \citep[Section~12]{arandaMECcompanion}. These cases illustrate how the measures order topics in the observed groups, but they do not provide a new expert benchmark or establish population-level polarization.

In August 2024, participants aged seventeen to twenty-five responded to eleven statements on a five-category agreement scale. MEC and ER both placed the statement about tougher measures against drug trafficking and insecurity in Cali lowest in polarization. They also agreed on the two highest-scoring statements, concerning non-traditional gender and family models and transgender participation in sport. They differed elsewhere: the statement about dialogue with armed groups ranked second from the least polarized under MEC and fifth under ER. This is a comparison of orderings within the demonstration, conditional on the statements and responses recorded there.

The late-2024 City Council exercise considered a metropolitan area, the planned 2025 budget, a proposed loan, and the mayor's management during 2024. Both measures assigned the lowest score to the metropolitan-area statement and the highest to the statement about the mayor's management. MEC placed the loan above the budget in polarization, while ER reversed those two. The figures thus document both agreement and disagreement between the measures on aggregate civic responses. On the four council histograms, the current reference implementations reproduce the displayed scores using $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,1.15}$ with the root normalization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:methods-root-normalization} and $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}$. This consistency check does not identify the historical code revision that generated the figures. The cases illustrate the measures' interpretations; they do not independently calibrate their parameters.

Together with the synthetic and expert comparisons, these illustrations address the descriptive use envisaged in the proposal. Section~\ref{subsec:discussion-objectives} relates the evidence to the mandatory activities, desirable extensions, and other methods in that plan.


\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

The research question joins two requirements: MEC should have a defensible mathematical interpretation of polarization, and its rankings should agree with expert judgments. The preceding results support these requirements to different extents. The proofs establish behavior throughout stated domains of distributions and parameters, whereas the experiments establish concordance on specified collections of profiles. This section examines what that evidence means and how far it fulfills the approved objectives.

\subsection{Interpreting the Theoretical and Empirical Evidence}\label{subsec:discussion-evidence}

The main theoretical contribution is to make resistance to consensus a precise object of measurement. The transport construction and weighted-dispersion characterization explain the effort being minimized. The maximality and axiomatic results then show how this interpretation behaves when groups consolidate, separate, or lose central mass. Together, these results give MEC a formal basis that extends beyond its numerical fit to particular profiles \citep{aranda2026mec}.

The original hypothesis nevertheless requires qualification. Joint compliance with the three Esteban--Ray axioms holds for $\alpha>1$ and $\beta\geq1$. Axiom~1 fails at $\alpha=1$, although Axioms~2 and~3 remain valid there. Central-split monotonicity holds throughout $\alpha,\beta\geq1$ under the symmetric configuration of Section~\ref{subsec:mec-central-split}. Thus the formal analysis establishes the anticipated properties with explicit conditions and corrects the proposal's parameter claim. Compliance with these axioms does not establish the stronger assumptions of the Kawada--Nakamura--Sunada characterization discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:lit-axioms}. MEC's compatibility with the original axiomatic framework and its difference from the pairwise ER construction can coexist.

This difference helps explain why the comparisons yield distinct rankings. ER aggregates antagonism between groups, whereas MEC evaluates the least effort needed to bring the whole distribution to agreement. The special cases connect this effort to familiar dispersion measures, while the general family changes the weight assigned to group size and displacement. The corpus matrix in Figure~\ref{fig:results-corpus-concordance} shows the empirical consequences of these choices. Concordance with another index describes similarity of rankings; it does not establish that either ranking is correct. A disagreement becomes informative when its underlying profiles and transformations can be explained.

The distinction between extremism and polarization is particularly useful here. The whole-group movement results and the Tipping Point analysis show why moving opinions farther from consensus can have different effects when an entire group moves and when only part of it moves. In the latter case, a longer displacement can accompany a reduction in MEC. The closed-form Tipping Point method is established for $\alpha=\beta=2$; it cannot be transferred directly to the empirical reference pair $(2,1.15)$. These results turn the proposal's conceptual distinction into a set of mathematical conditions, rather than a general rule that every move toward an extreme must raise polarization.

The expert comparison supplies a separate test of interpretation. On the fifteen published profiles, $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,1.15}$ matches the concordance of $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}$ and exceeds that of VdE and TWD. This supports its ability to recover the expert order on that benchmark. It partly supports the comparative hypothesis, since MEC does not improve on every tested alternative. Equal Kendall concordance also does not imply identical rankings, as the disagreements reported in Section~\ref{subsec:results-experts} show. The empirical result therefore supports a useful alternative interpretation of polarization without establishing universal superiority.

The optimal consensus point adds information to the scalar score, as it locates the least costly common position under the chosen masses, coordinates, and exponents. This supports the Minority Principle and the analysis of group movements in the paper. Its substantive meaning remains conditional on the model. An optimal point need not coincide with an available survey category, and at $\beta=1$ there may be an interval of optimal positions. Neither existence nor uniqueness predicts agreement among respondents or establishes that the calculated position is socially acceptable.

\subsection{Scope and Validity of the Findings}\label{subsec:discussion-validity}

The assessment of deliverables must be accompanied by an assessment of what the verified results can establish. The mathematical guarantees concern finite distributions on a one-dimensional interval. Applying the same formulas to network positions, several opinion dimensions, or general metric spaces would require a corresponding analysis of the domain and the consensus set. Even on an ordered survey scale, equal numerical spacing is an assumption about distance. The main experiments adopt that representation consistently, but agreement with the order of categories alone does not determine the distances between them.

Mass and parameter conventions also affect interpretation. Multiplying every mass by the same $\lambda>0$, while keeping the positions and exponents fixed, multiplies raw MEC by $\lambda^\alpha$. Comparisons between populations of different sizes therefore need an explicit convention. The normalized score provides a common range under the conditions stated in Section~\ref{subsec:methods-computation}. It does not remove dependence on the numerical representation or make distinct parameter pairs equivalent. The exponents specify how the model values group size and distance. Their interpretation does not establish that respondents' actual identification or costs of belief revision were measured.

The expert benchmark is small and consists of hypothetical histograms. The same profiles informed parameter selection and the reported concordance. The supplementary search confirms that many evaluated pairs attain the same maximum, so the benchmark does not identify a unique parametrization. Resampling and omissions describe sensitivity within these profiles. They do not provide an independent test on new profiles, new experts, or real survey populations. Search points with exponents below one fall outside the theoretical guarantees established here. The reference $(2,1.15)$ is therefore an empirically supported choice for this comparison, while a theoretical rationale for choosing these particular exponents remains open in the paper \citep{aranda2026mec}.

The exhaustive corpus serves a different purpose. It removes sampling from the enumeration of five-category count profiles with total $100$, making structural disagreements observable across that entire finite collection. It does not supply an external criterion of polarization and also does not cover other category spacings, totals, or support sizes. Reflection reduction preserves the score of each profile under reversal, but assigning one observation to each reflection class changes the multiplicities used in a correlation. The reported corpus must therefore remain part of the specification of the result.

Numerical conventions can affect ties and rankings. The rounding and truncation described in the methodology are distinct from solver accuracy, and neither is a theoretical feature of MEC. Small computational differences may matter when scores are close. The supplementary solver comparisons provide evidence on the checked profiles, while the public API returns an approximate minimum without the consensus point or termination record. Exact identities, numerical checks, and recorded settings provide complementary safeguards; none makes every unexamined calculation exact.

The performance evidence likewise has a defined scope. ER is faster in the saved five-category corpus timing, whereas both MEC solvers are faster at the larger tested support sizes beginning at $400$ in the original eleven-size workload. These results concern the implementations and settings examined. They do not establish a universal crossover or make the proposal's laptop-feasibility claim an observed runtime for every experiment. Algorithmic operation counts and measured elapsed times answer related but different questions.

\subsection{Interpretation and Responsible Use}\label{sec:ethics}\label{subsec:discussion-use}

The proposal's concern with diagnosis, monitoring, and evaluation motivates measurement, but the present evidence supports descriptive comparisons of opinion distributions. A score and its histogram can help distinguish concentration, separation, and imbalance. A change in the score alone does not identify its cause, demonstrate the effect of an intervention, or forecast social conflict. Interpreting a result requires the survey question, response scale, population, mass convention, and parameter choice. Threshold labels additionally require evidence for their calibration and suitability in the setting where they are used.

The main evaluation uses synthetic profiles and published aggregate expert scores. No new expert judgments were collected. The regional illustrations summarize previously documented aggregate comparisons from the team's applications. The analysis does not use respondent-level records to profile individuals. This retains the proposal's limited data footprint without treating every aggregate dataset as automatically anonymous. Further use of the regional observations must respect their provenance, conditions of use, and disclosure risks. Restricted records remain separate from the public code and synthetic artifacts.

The open implementation, license, test workflow, and documented experiments support scrutiny of the measurement procedure. They do not substitute for the contextual evidence needed to interpret a civic application. The same applies to $y^\star$. A least costly consensus under the model is a structural result; using it in mediation or policy would require a substantive account of whose positions and costs it represents. The paper identifies such applications as questions for further investigation. Broader applications require their own contextual evidence.


\section{Conclusions and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}\label{sec:contrib}

\subsection{Conclusions}\label{subsec:conclusion-findings}

This thesis has presented the mathematical and computational development of MEC reported in the joint paper \citep{aranda2026mec}, in relation to the approved research objectives. The results establish resistance to consensus as a precise interpretation of polarization for finite opinion distributions on $[0,1]$ and also support strong agreement with expert judgments on the evaluated benchmark. The theoretical and empirical parts of the research question thus receive a qualified answer, with the conditions of the proofs and the scope of the comparisons determining what can be concluded.

The formal contribution connects transportation to consensus, convex optimization, and weighted dispersion within one parametric family. MEC is zero exactly at consensus, and the balanced extreme distribution maximizes it among distributions with the same total mass for $\alpha,\beta\geq1$. Optimal consensus points exist throughout this domain and are unique when $\beta>1$. The case $(1,1)$ is the minimum Earth Mover's Distance to a consensus distribution of the same total mass. For unit-mass distributions, $(1,1)$ and $(1,2)$ give mean absolute deviation about a median and variance, respectively. For general total mass, these dispersions are multiplied by $\Sigma\pi$. The general family permits separate sensitivity to group size and distance.

The axiomatic analysis establishes joint compliance with the three original Esteban--Ray axioms for $\alpha>1$ and $\beta\geq1$. It also establishes central-split monotonicity under its stated symmetric configuration, throughout $\alpha,\beta\geq1$. These results refine the proposal's hypothesis and supply explicit grounds for interpreting MEC as a polarization measure. They do not establish the stronger Kawada--Nakamura--Sunada characterization or equivalence with ER.

The optimal consensus point gives the measure additional structure and supports the Minority Principle for two-point distributions and the analysis of whole-group movements. The Tipping Point method for $\alpha=\beta=2$ further explains why a partial transfer toward an extreme may initially reduce polarization. Group size, separation, and the amount transferred must be considered together. The distinction between extremism and polarization is therefore supported by mathematical results, rather than left as a verbal distinction.

The empirical contribution locates MEC among competing measures through the exhaustive five-point corpus and the fifteen published expert-rated profiles. On the expert benchmark, $\mathrm{MEC}_{2,1.15}$ attains Kendall's $\tau_B\approx0.886$, matching $\mathrm{ER}_{0.8}$ and exceeding VdE and TWD in the reported comparison. The hypothesis of improved agreement is partly supported. Parameter selection and evaluation use the same profiles, so this result establishes concordance on that benchmark without establishing independent validity on unseen data. The supplementary search supports the finding that several evaluated parameter pairs achieve the same concordance.

The implementation supplies the computational part of this development. The Python API, tests, comparison scripts, and saved outputs allow the numerical procedures and reported comparisons to be examined. Analytical identities and reference computations provide checks on the optimization. These contributions develop the proposal's three central aims of formal grounding, an empirical comparison, and reproducible infrastructure. Section~\ref{subsec:discussion-objectives} distinguishes this completed core from the software deliverables whose completion remains unverified, the desirable extensions, and the wider methodological plan. The regional figures form part of my contribution to the team's applications, rather than an individual claim to their full analysis.

The resulting contribution is a measure whose value has a stated geometric meaning and whose behavior can be examined through proofs and computations. Its strongest conclusion concerns the finite one-dimensional model and the profiles actually evaluated. The evidence supports MEC as a mathematically grounded and empirically promising approach to measuring opinion polarization, with a clear basis for further investigation.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{subsec:conclusion-future}

Several directions remain open in the paper \citep{aranda2026mec}. The first is to extend the formulation beyond finite distributions on $[0,1]$. Replacing $|x-y|$ with a distance in a general metric space would allow a broader representation of opinions. Such an extension would require examining the consensus domain, existence and uniqueness of optima, and which polarization properties survive. The multidimensional and network settings envisaged beyond the proposal's scope belong to this line of inquiry.

A second direction concerns the dependence on $\alpha$ and $\beta$. The benchmark supports $(2,1.15)$ as one successful setting, but does not give a theoretical reason for that choice or identify a unique optimum. Studying parameter sensitivity together with the axiomatic requirements could clarify how to choose exponents for a specified measurement task. Extending the Tipping Point method beyond $\alpha=\beta=2$ would provide a related account of partial mass transfers across the family.

Comparisons on independent collections of profiles and real survey distributions could test whether the expert concordance persists beyond the profiles used for parameter selection. They could also study sensitivity to sampling uncertainty, noise, and the numerical spacing assigned to ordered categories. This develops the proposal's interest in uncertainty while extending the paper's empirical evidence beyond its hypothetical benchmark.

The paper also identifies opinion-update models as a setting for further study. Applying MEC along a sequence of modeled distributions could clarify how its value changes during opinion evolution. Such a study would need an explicit dynamic model, since the static consensus optimum alone does not determine an eventual agreement or the time needed to reach it.

\enlargethispage{\baselineskip} Finally, the substantive interpretation of $y^\star$ in mediation and policy framing remains open. The present research treats it as a structural object determined by the effort function. Exploring an applied interpretation would require evidence about what the modeled masses, distances, and costs represent in the setting concerned. This is a question about the connection between the mathematical model and collective deliberation, in addition to the computation of the optimum.

\section{Uniqueness of the Tipping Point}\label{app:mec-tipping-uniqueness}

This appendix supplies the algebraic step used in Theorem~\ref{th:mec-tipping-point}. It follows the uniqueness proof in Appendix~F of the completed paper \citep{aranda2026mec}. No uniqueness claim for the general-support cubic of Proposition~\ref{prop:mec-general-transfer} is inferred from it.

\begin{proof}[Proof of uniqueness in Theorem~\ref{th:mec-tipping-point}]
Let $m,n,d,e>0$, and put $u=n^2/m^2$, $s=e/d$, and $t=k/n$. Then
\[
R_{m,n,d,e}(k)=d^2m^4n\,\widetilde R_{u,s}(t),
\]
where
\[
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde R_{u,s}(t)
={}&s^2u(u+1)t^3-2s^2u(u+1)t^2\\
 &+\bigl(s^2(u+1)^2+2s(u+1)+2\bigr)t-2.
\end{aligned}
\]
The prefactor is positive, and $k\in(0,n)$ is equivalent to $t\in(0,1)$. The endpoint values are $\widetilde R_{u,s}(0)=-2$ and $\widetilde R_{u,s}(1)=s(u+1)(s+2)>0$.

The discriminant with respect to $t$ is
\[
\operatorname{Disc}_t(\widetilde R_{u,s})
=-4s^2u(u+1)\,\mathcal{P}(u,s),
\]
where
\[
\mathcal{P}(u,s)
=(s^6+2s^5)u^5+u^2(Au^2+Bu+C)+Du+E,
\]
and
\[
\begin{aligned}
A&=5s^6+14s^5+8s^4,\\
B&=10s^6+36s^5+26s^4-12s^3,\\
C&=10s^6+44s^5+46s^4+8s^3+23s^2,\\
D&=5s^6+26s^5+46s^4+52s^3+59s^2+24s,\\
E&=s^6+6s^5+18s^4+32s^3+36s^2+24s+8.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here $A,C,D,E>0$. Moreover,
\[
4AC-B^2
=4s^6(s+2)
 \bigl(25s^5+130s^4+212s^3+204s^2+234s+74\bigr)>0.
\]
The quadratic $Au^2+Bu+C$ is therefore positive for every real $u$. All remaining terms in $\mathcal{P}(u,s)$ are positive for $u,s>0$, so the discriminant is strictly negative.

A real cubic with negative discriminant has one real root and two nonreal conjugate roots. Its nonzero discriminant also ensures that the real root is simple. The opposite endpoint signs put that unique real root in $(0,1)$, hence the unique root of $R_{m,n,d,e}$ lies in $(0,n)$.
\end{proof}
